# Chapter 3. Nature has no bosses

*A working draft, November 2026. This book is being written while The Grove is being built; expect it to change.*

---

> *I was twelve when I first wrote code, on a ZX81 with a keyboard you had to press like a doorbell. I loved it. I still do. Like a lot of people who love building things, I was ambitious too, so the path seemed obvious: freelance first, then a company of my own. In time it became a software consultancy that grew far bigger than that twelve-year-old could have imagined.*
>
> *Somewhere along the way I learned something nobody had warned me about. The people who knew our customers best were the ones furthest from where the decisions were made. I'd sit with my opposite number in a client's organisation, two people at the top, both a little out of touch, trying to steer something we could no longer see clearly. They had the same problem I did. My mentors told me that's simply how it works: someone has to be at the top, and the top has to decide. It never sat comfortably. I knew it was wrong. I just didn't know what right looked like.*

Ask most people how a large group gets anything done and you'll hear the same answer. Someone has to be in charge.

It sounds like common sense. We've grown up with it. Families have heads, schools have heads, companies have chief executives, countries have presidents. When something goes wrong, the first question is who was responsible, and the second is who should have stopped it. If nobody is in charge, the thinking goes, things fall apart: work gets dropped, people pull in different directions, and the loudest voice wins.

That worry isn't foolish. Most of us have seen a team lose its leader with nobody stepping into the gap. Meetings go round in circles. Decisions drift. The people who care most end up carrying everyone else. And if you've worked somewhere that called itself "flat", you'll know that taking away the boss doesn't take away the need for direction. It just hides it.

So this chapter doesn't argue that nobody should ever lead. It asks a narrower question, and an older one. Is "someone in charge" the only way a large group can act as one? Or is it just the only way we know?

## What the boss is really for

Strip the title away and a boss in most organisations is doing three jobs.

The first is **knowing**. Information travels up: what customers said, what broke, what's running late. The boss gathers it, because nobody else can see the whole.

The second is **deciding**. With the whole in view, the boss chooses what matters, who does what, and what waits.

The third is **telling**. The decision travels back down, and people act on it.

That loop made sense for most of history, when information moved slowly and only a few people could see across an organisation. It still works, after a fashion. But look at what it costs.

Every decision waits for one person's attention. The people closest to the problem, who usually know most, have to explain it upwards and then wait. By the time an answer comes back, the situation has moved on. The person at the top, meanwhile, is drowning: too much coming in, too little time, and the quiet dread of being the one who'll be blamed. The people below learn, slowly, that their job is to report and comply rather than to think. Some of the best of them leave. The rest stop offering what they see.

> *Something else became clear as we grew. Small groups worked. With a handful of people, everyone could see what everyone else was doing, and things moved. Somewhere around eight, that began to change. People formed their own little silos, and the real conversations moved to the water cooler. By twelve the transparency was gone, and I was increasingly in the dark about what was actually happening. At that size structure becomes necessary, or people drift. The only structure I knew was another layer of someone-in-charge, and every layer put the people at the top a little further from the customer.*

None of this is anyone's fault. It's the shape of the system, and the shape came from a time when there was no other way to get information to where decisions were made.

Around the same time, in another software company on the other side of the Atlantic, Brian Robertson was wrestling with the same frustration and beginning to build the practice that became Holacracy: authority given to roles rather than people, and a way for anyone to change the structure when they sensed it wasn't working.[^1] It would have answered many of the questions I was asking. It was ten years before I found it. Even then, as we'll see in later chapters, something was still missing.

## Where to look

> *So where do you look, on the days when the problem won't let go and there's nobody in the building to share it with? For me the answer was long walks, and books: systems thinking, Spiral Dynamics, Margaret Wheatley, whose work on organisations and the new science I read from cover to cover. Somewhere between the walking and the reading, I started looking more closely at how the living world around me was organised, and who, if anyone, was in charge.*

## The slime mould and the railway

In 2010, a team of Japanese and British researchers did something that sounds like a children's science project. They took a plate shaped like the area around Tokyo, put oat flakes where the surrounding cities sit, and placed a slime mould where Tokyo itself would be.[^2]

A slime mould is about as simple as life gets. No brain, no nerves, no centre of any kind. It's a spreading yellow film that feels its way across a surface looking for food.

Within about a day it had reached every oat flake. Then it began to thin out the paths that weren't carrying much and thicken the ones that were. What was left was a network of tubes linking the "cities" that looked remarkably like the real railway network around Tokyo, the one engineers spent decades planning. On some measures it was about as efficient, and in some ways more resilient when a link was cut.

Nothing in the slime mould planned that. Each part sensed what was around it, grew towards what fed it, and let go of what didn't. The design came out of the sensing.

## Traces in the mud

Termites build mounds taller than a person, with chambers, tunnels and air that keeps itself cool. Not one termite holds the drawing.

In the 1950s a French biologist, Pierre-Paul Grassé, set out to understand how. He found that termites don't coordinate by agreeing a plan. They coordinate through the work itself. A termite drops a ball of mud carrying a little scent. Others are more likely to drop theirs where mud already lies. A small pile becomes a bigger pile, then a pillar, and pillars that lean towards each other become an arch. Grassé called this *stigmergy*: work guided by the traces of earlier work.[^3]

It reverses the usual order. In most organisations someone decides, and then the work follows. In a termite mound, the work leaves a trail, and the trail decides what happens next.

## No ant gives orders

Harvester ants, studied for decades by Deborah Gordon in the deserts of Arizona, have a queen. But the queen doesn't rule. She lays eggs. No ant tells any other ant what to do.[^4]

Yet a colony adjusts how many ants go out foraging from minute to minute, according to how much food there is, how hot it is, and how dangerous the day looks. It does this through something as plain as how often ants bump into each other. A forager returning with a seed brushes past an ant waiting at the entrance. If foragers come back often, food is plentiful and more ants go out. If they come back rarely, fewer do. Each ant follows a simple local rule, and the colony as a whole responds to its world.

Starlings do something similar in the sky. A murmuration of thousands of birds turns as one, and for a long time people assumed there must be a leader. There isn't. Researchers who tracked individual birds found that each starling attends to a handful of its nearest neighbours, about six or seven, and adjusts to them.[^5] That's enough for the whole flock to move together, and to swerve away from a falcon faster than any single bird could signal.

Honeybees go further: they make decisions. When a colony needs a new home, a few hundred scouts fly out and come back to dance for the sites they've found. Better sites get longer, livelier dances, which recruit more scouts, who go and look for themselves. When enough scouts have gathered at one site, the swarm moves. Thomas Seeley, who spent years watching this, called it honeybee democracy: many independent views, open debate, and a decision that comes when a quorum agrees.[^6]

## Even the wolves

You might be thinking of the obvious exception. Surely wolves have an alpha.

The "alpha wolf" came largely from studies of captive wolves, unrelated animals forced together in an enclosure, who did fight for dominance. In 1999 L. David Mech, the biologist whose own early book had helped spread the idea, published a paper saying, in effect, that he'd got it wrong. In the wild, a wolf pack is mostly a family. The leaders are the parents. They lead the way parents do, through experience and care, and the young leave to start packs of their own.[^7]

We tell ourselves nature is organised like our organisations. Often it's the other way round. We're so surrounded by hierarchy that we see it everywhere, even where it isn't.

## What nature is doing instead

Put these together and a pattern shows through.

- **Sensing happens everywhere, not at the top.** The slime mould feels with every edge. Every ant reads its own encounters.
- **The work leaves traces, and the traces guide.** Termites follow the mud. Ants follow the rate of return.
- **Decisions come from many local choices, with a threshold.** Bees move when a quorum agrees, not when a leader says so.
- **Each part has a job, and none is in charge.** The Portuguese man o' war, which looks like one jellyfish, is a colony of separate organisms, each specialised for floating, catching, eating or reproducing, working as one body with no single part in control.

None of this means nature is gentle, or that it has no structure. It has a great deal of structure. It just doesn't keep it in one place.

## How we decided

I didn't come to this as a blank sheet. Hierarchy had already failed me in practice, in exactly the way described above. I'd seen flat organisations fail too: without structure, the most confident voices take over, and the structure that forms is invisible and unaccountable. And I'd worked inside Holacracy, which got closer than anything else, giving authority to roles and letting anyone change the structure, but which still leaned on people running its meetings and keeping its records by hand.

> *In time the ideas needed gathering, and they gathered into a story. I wrote *Max Unleashed*, a fable about a small mouse with his heart in the right place, who learns from the living world around him how to solve his problems and build something fairer, with no one in charge. Telling it as a tale made the idea whole for the first time. What was left was to make it real. I hope the story can still do for others what it did for me: show a new way before anyone asks how it works.*

What I couldn't do, for a long time, was build the alternative. A termite has its mud and a bee has its dance. People in an organisation needed something that could carry the traces, hold the structure and keep the agreements, without a person at the top doing it for them. And it was simply too big for one person to hold, the ideas and the code together.

Then the technology arrived. It became possible to try something, throw it away and start again within days, and to work through ideas at a pace no one person could manage. So I brought the idea to the "we", and we began to work on it together. Years of solo effort were compressed into weeks. The question wasn't whether to move beyond hierarchy. It was how far to follow nature, and what to keep.

Copying nature outright was never the answer. Nature is ruthless at the edges. Parts that don't pull their weight are starved or shed. A colony that chooses badly dies. We didn't want an organisation that treats its people the way a slime mould treats a dead-end tube.

So we borrowed nature's principles and kept the things that make us human. From nature we took sensing at the edges, work that leaves a trail, structure that grows where the work needs it and withers where it doesn't, and decisions that move when enough of the right people agree. To that we added what nature doesn't offer: a floor so nobody is starved, the right to leave at any time, consent so that nobody is overridden, and a purpose people choose together. How we built it is the story of the chapters that follow.

We're still learning where the line sits. Some of what nature does well, like letting go of what no longer serves, is hard for people. We'll come back to that.

## What you might take from this

If you work in an organisation with a boss, nothing here says your boss is the problem. The job your boss is doing, knowing, deciding and telling, just isn't the only way a group can act as one. Life has been running a different way for a very long time.

The question for the rest of this book isn't whether we can do without anyone leading. It's this: if a slime mould can design a railway, what could a group of thoughtful people do, given the right way to sense, to leave traces for each other, and to decide together?
