# Chapter 18. Into the legal world

*A working draft, November 2026. This book is being written while the operating system is being built; expect it to change. Checked against the build as it stood on 1 November 2026.*

Sooner or later every conversation about organisations without bosses reaches the same wall.

You can design all the forums and clocks you like, the sceptic says. The day you sign a lease, open a bank account or take someone on, the law asks for a name. A director. A signatory. Someone who can be sued. The legal entity wins in the end. Somewhere there's a person who signs, and whatever your software says, that person is the boss.

It's a fair worry, and plenty of people have learned it the hard way. A company that runs on circles still has a board, and a board can dissolve the circles with one resolution. A founder who gave away power still owns the shares, and on the day of a sale the shares speak. Holacracy is adopted when someone with authority ratifies its constitution, and what they ratified they can take back. Self-management, in most places, lives on borrowed authority.

## What the paper costs

A legal body isn't a formality. It's how an organisation holds a contract, a bank account, a lease or a piece of land. It's what stands between the members and personal liability: without one, a group acting together can be treated by a court as a partnership, with each person exposed for what the others did. It's who the tax authorities write to. Banks won't open an account for a good intention.

So nearly every organisation takes the usual form, and with it the usual shape of authority. Authority starts in the legal body, with the owners and the board, and is handed down. Whatever self-management happens below is a permission.

The people inside feel that, even when nobody says it. They know the circles can be overruled, so the circles start to feel like theatre. They learn to check with whoever signs. The person whose name is on the bank account becomes the person everyone asks, whatever the structure chart says. And when that person leaves, the organisation finds out how much hung on one name: the account still in their name, the login only they knew.

It costs the signer too. They carry legal exposure for decisions they made with others, or didn't make at all. Many of them end up holding back the very freedom they wanted to give, because they're the one who'd answer for it.

The law isn't blind to other ways. Co-operatives have carried shared ownership for well over a century, on principles the International Co-operative Alliance set down as voluntary membership and democratic member control.[^1] In the UK, employee ownership trusts hold a company's shares for the people who work there, with tax relief introduced after a government review in 2012.[^2] Both are real achievements, and both show that the law can carry something other than a single owner. They also show the gap. They change who owns the organisation. They don't, on their own, change how it decides. A co-operative elects a board; an employee-owned company still has directors who direct. Ownership and authority are different questions, and the legal forms mostly answer the first.

## A passport, not a new king

The turn is to stop asking whether to have a legal body and ask which way authority flows through it.

And to see the legal body as part of the organisation, not something standing outside or above it. It's how the organisation moves in the real world: how it signs a lease, holds an account, employs someone, is seen by a court. It's optional, and it belongs to the organisation the way a branch does. Here it's a collectively stewarded legal entity: owned by the organisation, stewarded by its members together, carrying out what the organisation decides, and answering to it.

In the usual company, the paper is the source and the organisation borrows from it. Turn that round. Decisions are made in the organisation, by the people doing the work, in the way the earlier chapters describe. The legal body doesn't decide anything. It points at where decisions are made and says to a bank or a court: what they agreed, we agreed.

Until recently that was a nice idea with nowhere to stand. In 2021 the Wyoming legislature passed what's usually called its DAO supplement, which lets a limited liability company declare itself a decentralised autonomous organisation and name the smart contract, the running code, that governs it.[^3] (A DAO is simply an organisation whose rules are carried out by code its members agreed, rather than by an officer.) The Marshall Islands passed its own law the following year.[^4] For the first time, the paper could say: the rules are over there, in the running system.

An organisation running on the operating system is already a DAO in that sense, from its first day, as Chapter 6 described: its rules run as code its people agreed. What it doesn't have, until it takes a legal form, is a body the law can see.

That's the shape organisations running on the operating system are designed to take. We call it a passport, not a new king. A passport lets you travel and be recognised. It doesn't tell you where to go.

## What the design says

None of this is built yet, so read this section as design.

Taking on a legal body is optional, and each organisation chooses for itself. An organisation that never signs a contract, holds property or banks money in its own name never needs one, and many begin without, keeping their books beside whatever account they already use. When an organisation does take one on, it's a proposal for the whole organisation, heard at the root, with a timelock before it takes effect, because it changes what the organisation is. Its opposite, winding the legal body up, is the same kind of proposal.

The legal body has two layers, never one. The first is a DAO limited liability company, owned by the organisation itself, which its members govern. Not unowned: the company is the organisation's, and the organisation owns what it holds. Members aren't allocated shares in it. Nothing prevents an organisation doing that, but nothing in the operating system enables it. The second is a purpose lock beside it. The reason is simple. A bare company is only as strong as its members, and a captured majority could vote to sell everything and share out the proceeds. The lock means that what the organisation has set aside for its purpose can't be cashed out, even by its members. Capture can seize the vote; it can't empty the vault.

The operating agreement, the company's founding document, does three things deliberately. It names the organisation's running rules as the governing contract, so people joining, leaving, taking seats and stepping down never means filing new paper. It separates the right to a say from the right to money, because a company's default share fuses the two, and organisations on The Grove keep them apart: a say comes from the trust peers place in you, and money never buys one. And it writes the jury drawn by lot, the last resort from Chapter 11, in as its arbitration clause, so that a court asked to settle a dispute is pointed back to the organisation's own process. For the first organisation that came to us, we added terms its members can't later vote away: seats never carry money, money never carries a vote, and nobody can raise the cap on an investor's return.

## The person who signs

Here the sceptic is partly right, and we'd rather say so.

The law needs natural persons. A DAO company needs an organiser, who files the papers and keeps nothing afterwards; a registered agent, which is usually a hired address; and, for a United States company, a responsible party on the tax registration, a named human who is said to control it.

Our answer is that this is a role, not power. The form records control; it doesn't grant it. The person holding the role owns nothing by holding it, and has no authority beyond what the organisation's own decisions already give them. They can be replaced. Their nationality doesn't matter. We designed it as accountability that points down, to the members, never as a lever that points up. A Marshall Islands company doesn't ask for a responsible party at all, which is part of why we lean there.

The acts the legal body performs in the world, forming itself, binding an asset under the lock, winding up, are designed to run through the system rather than through anyone's hands, on the same principle as every other connection: no person holds the key.

What we haven't designed is the everyday signature. A landlord, a supplier or a bank will sometimes want a human hand on a page. How that person is chosen, how far their signature reaches, and how each act is tied back to the decision that allowed it, is open. One shape we've sketched is a seat like any other: offered, consented to, scoped to what's been decided, free to step down from, every signature recorded where everyone can see it. That's a sketch, not a design. And we'd be glossing over something if we didn't add that a signature is real power in the moment it's made. An operating agreement can make a rogue signature a breach, and a jury can rule on it afterwards. Paper is enforced after the fact.

## Where things stand

What's built is narrow. Money stays in the organisation's own bank, as Chapter 17 described, and the books sit beside it; nothing in the system holds or moves money. There's a register of the things an organisation holds, written to mirror what a legal body's books would hold. And there's a socket in the core of the system where a real legal wrapper will plug in, able to form a body and bind an asset under the lock, with a stand-in for testing. No real organisation has been wrapped through it. The proposals to take on and wind up a legal body are designed, not built.

One early organisation chose a Marshall Islands DAO company, and a pathway towards it rather than a leap: an agreement between the members now, and forming the company at the first of several triggers, such as a first contract or bank account, outside money, or a date. Until the organisation runs on the live system, an operating agreement can't name it, so the plan is for the members' decisions to be recorded in writing and switched over by amendment later. And members living in different countries still carry their own countries' reporting duties, whatever the company's home.

Several things are open. An organisation on The Grove can end or divide; a company carries on until someone winds it up, so the design needs a step that winds it up, or the organisation leaves behind a legal zombie with filings due. Whether an ending starts that step by itself is open (our lean: it starts it, and a person completes the filing). When an organisation divides, which half keeps the existing company, and how things run during the months a legal split takes, is open. So is the case of an organisation growing inside an existing company, whose board still holds the law's authority and whose bank account still has human signatories. We haven't designed how that board's ratification would work.

The design also routes privacy through the legal body: personal details are meant to be kept where they can be deleted, with only proof that something happened kept for good, and each country's duty to erase met through the company. That too is designed, not built.

And a plain word: none of this is legal advice. Laws move, and an organisation taking a legal form needs its own counsel.

## How we decided

We weighed five paths. No legal body at all leaves members exposed and banks unwilling. An ordinary company whose board promises to follow the organisation's decisions is where most self-managing organisations live, and it works until the board changes its mind. A co-operative is tried and trusted, but it builds in an elected board and one member, one vote, where organisations on The Grove decide by consent weighted by trust. A foundation with no members is heavier than a small organisation needs. A DAO company with a purpose lock was the only one where the paper could point at the organisation rather than sit above it.

Between jurisdictions, Wyoming is cheap and written for DAOs, but it pulls a scattered membership into United States federal tax and needs a responsible party. The Marshall Islands has neither, at the cost of a single formation provider and harder questions from banks. The jurisdiction is a setting, not a principle; it will change as the law does.

Thomas Thomison's PowerShift has faced the same wall and answered it differently, and well.[^5] It too uses a Wyoming DAO company, and it names a permanent human Orchestrator who ratifies binding acts. That has real strengths. It's honest about what the law needs. Banks and courts can see exactly who answers. Nobody is pretending the signature isn't there. Where we part company is the word permanent. A standing ratifier is a seat others learn to wait for, and that's how a boss grows back. We'd rather the human anchor be replaceable and hold nothing beyond what decisions give. But theirs is a working answer and ours, at the point of the everyday signature, isn't yet. A fair reader could conclude that until we've designed it, the named Orchestrator is the more honest of the two.

What's still uncertain: whether banks will be patient with a crypto-linked company; whether a court will defer to a jury clause in practice; whether someone holding a responsible party role with no extra say will feel that's a fair deal for the exposure; and how the everyday signature will work at all.

## What you might take from this

The legal world asks for a name, and that's no reason to give up self-management. The question is which way authority flows. If the paper points at where the organisation decides, rather than sitting above it, a person can hold the legal role without holding the power. The signature still needs a human, and that part deserves more honesty than it usually gets.

A legal body can be a passport for the organisation, not a new king over it.
