Society is just people. There's nothing up there to talk to.

That's the doubt, and it sounds like common sense. There are eight billion of us, each getting on with a life. Add us up and you get a lot of people, some laws and some habits. Nobody is in charge of the whole, and there's no one to ask what it wants. Talk of "what society is telling us" sounds like a metaphor at best and mysticism at worst.

This chapter takes the other view, carefully. It isn't a mystical one. It's closer to plain observation, and some of the most careful thinkers of the last hundred years got there first.

## The nesting doesn't stop at the person

Atoms make molecules. Molecules make cells. Cells make organs, and organs make a body. Each level is a whole in its own right and a part of the next one up. Arthur Koestler called each of these a holon, a whole that is also a part, and the whole ladder a holarchy.[^1] Holacracy took its name from the idea.[^2] Chapter 5 comes back to it, because it describes how a living organisation is put together.

The usual picture stops at the person, as if a human being were the top of the ladder. But a person is a part of things too: a family, an organisation, a town, a society, a species living on one planet. So the question is fair. If the nesting carries on above us, what's up there, and what is it doing?

## What emerges, and presses back

Here's one answer. Every day, each of us does small things. We buy this and not that. We share one post and scroll past another. We say yes to a deadline we know is unreasonable, because everyone does. We vote, or don't. None of these acts is large. But they add up, and what they add up to doesn't stay put. It comes back to us as the world we live in: prices, norms, the mood of a country, what feels normal, what feels impossible.

Émile Durkheim, one of sociology's founders, called these social facts: things none of us made alone that still press on all of us, as real as the weather.[^3] Peter Berger and Thomas Luckmann described the loop almost exactly in the 1960s. We put things out into the world. They harden into "how things are". Then we take them back in as reality, and forget we made them.[^4] Anthony Giddens said the same of structure: we make it through what we do, and it shapes what we do next.[^5]

So society isn't just people. It's people, plus the patterns our actions leave behind, plus the way those patterns act back on us.

## An intelligence with no one home

Is it fair to call that an intelligence? In one sense, yes. Friedrich Hayek pointed out that a market pulls together knowledge no single person has, and turns it into prices that guide millions of decisions.[^6] Thomas Malone at MIT calls markets, democracies and hierarchies "superminds": groups of people, and now machines, acting together in ways that look intelligent.[^7] Pierre Lévy gave collective intelligence its name.[^8] Further out, Pierre Teilhard de Chardin pictured a layer of thought wrapping the planet, and Barbara Marx Hubbard pictured humanity as a single organism slowly becoming aware of itself.[^9]

One caution keeps this honest. Ken Wilber warned against treating a society as a bigger version of a person.[^10] Your cells are parts of you; you aren't a part of society in the same way. You're a member, and you can leave. And there's no "I" up there looking out. A society senses, adapts and acts back on us, but nobody is home.

That caution sharpens the point rather than weakening it. What sits above us is intelligence without awareness. It takes in everything we do and hands back the result, and it has no idea what it's doing.

## A mirror we don't look into

Carl Jung used the word shadow for the parts of ourselves we don't see, and so act out without choosing to.[^11] What emerges from all our small actions is something like a shadow of ours. It acts on us, constantly, and we rarely look at it as ours.

Chapter 1 described the trap of races to the bottom, where everyone does what nobody wants because nobody can afford to stop first. That's what the shadow looks like when it hardens. Otto Scharmer puts it plainly: we keep collectively creating results that nobody wants.[^12] We meet those results as the news, as the state of things, as something done to us. What we almost never do is ask what they're showing us about ourselves, and how we might feed them differently.

## The mirror bends

There's a twist. The mirror isn't flat. A social feed tuned to keep people scrolling doesn't show us who we are. It shows us an amplified version of whatever held our attention. One study found that people who were rewarded with likes for expressing outrage went on to express more of it.[^13] We look into a mirror that exaggerates our worst moments, and then take what we see as the truth about us.

So there are two questions, not one. How could we feed the collective differently? And who shapes the mirror, and could it be shaped differently? Chapter 1 argued that the coordination failure isn't a moral failing. The same holds here. The mirror reflects how we're organised as much as who we are.

## A liver in a body

None of us can see the whole from where we stand. A liver does its work faithfully, but it can't see the body it belongs to. It can't tell whether the body is running, sleeping or falling ill. It only knows its own small part of the work.

That's our position in society. And it's why exhortation doesn't work. Telling people to see the bigger picture asks a liver to see the body.

But a body can have a sense of itself that every part shares in. And an organisation is a holon in the middle, big enough to have a collective intelligence of its own, small enough that its loop can close. Inside one organisation, what everyone does can be read as it happens, the work, the promises, the trust, the tensions, and reflected back openly to everyone in it. People can then respond where they stand, and the next reflection carries what they did. That's the shadow made visible, at a scale where people can answer to it.

## The mirror that talks back

Until recently, a reading of an organisation was something you had to know how to look at: a chart, a report, a dashboard someone had to interpret. AI changes that. A model can take what the reading shows, put it into plain words, and answer questions about it. Nobody has to be an analyst to ask how things are. Anyone in the organisation can ask, and the organisation can talk back. Chapter 21 describes the sense of itself we're building towards, and Chapter 22 the voice that puts it into words.

The same move works a level up. Organisations on The Grove are holons too, parts of a network of organisations. If each one can sense itself, the network can be read in the same way, without anyone's records being collected: where needs go unmet, where the work the world sends is shifting, where strain is gathering across many organisations at once. That would be the beginning of a read back from society itself, or at least from the part of it organised this way. For the first time, the layer above us wouldn't only act on us. We could ask it how it's doing, and what our small actions are adding up to.

None of this is built. It's the direction the whole design points in.

> *That's what I'm reaching for. If the organisation's sense of itself works, we'll be able to talk to it, and it will tell us how our collective intelligence is doing. Do the same across enough organisations, and a little of society starts to speak back too. For once we'd get to look in the mirror and ask what it's showing us.*

> *I came to holons sideways. I was in my kitchen in Suffolk, standing at the island while something cooked, reading everything I could find about Holacracy. There it was: the ladder from atoms up to people, each level a whole and a part. The organisation was clearly one of those levels. What caught me was the next step up. If it doesn't stop at us, what's above? I thought society, and then I wondered whether society was a kind of collective intelligence, made of all our little actions and pressing back on us as the world we experience. And because we don't think of it that way, it works in the dark. It shows us ourselves, and we never ask what it's showing us. Much of what I've built since is an attempt to give an organisation the chance to see itself, and to answer.*

## How we decided

This chapter is the reason for much of what follows. We chose to read the work, the promises, the trust and the tensions, rather than to measure people, because the reading is how an organisation can see the pattern its people make together. Chapter 19 describes that read, and Chapter 21 the learned sense of itself we're building towards.

We chose to make the reading open to everyone, rather than a view for whoever sits at the top, because a mirror only one person can look into is a management tool, not a sense of self.

And we chose to start with the organisation rather than with society. Society is too large for any of us to see, and too large for its loop to close in a way people can feel. An organisation isn't. If enough organisations learn to see themselves and respond, the layer above them might begin to change too. Chapter 30 picks that up.

## What you might take from this

Society isn't something that happens to us. It's what all our small actions add up to, handed back. It acts like an intelligence with no one home, and because we don't look at it as ours, it runs in the dark.

We can't see the whole from where we stand. But we can build places where the whole speaks back, first an organisation, then a network of them, and in time perhaps something of society itself.
