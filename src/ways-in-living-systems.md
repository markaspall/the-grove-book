If you think in systems, you've probably spent years watching organisations treat themselves as machines. Restructures drawn on a whiteboard. People as parts. Plans that miss because the world moved while they were being written. You've read Meadows on leverage points, Capra on the web of life, Beer on viable systems, de Geus on why a few companies live for centuries. And you've probably noticed how little of it reaches the software organisations actually run on, which is mostly forms, tickets and dashboards.

This book asks what an organisation would look like if we took the living picture seriously, and built it.

## What you'll find here

**A pattern, not a machine.** An organisation is more like a whirlpool than a building: a pattern that keeps re-forming around a purpose while what flows through it changes. It has many boundaries rather than one, like a membrane, and parts that are also wholes. Each branch carries its own purpose and can grow, split, join and fade.

**Nature's moves, kept human.** Slime moulds, termites, starlings and bees coordinate with nobody in charge, by sensing at the edges, leaving traces that guide, and deciding at a threshold. The book borrows those moves, adds what keeps them human, and is clear about where it parts from nature.

**Sensing without surveillance.** The organisation reads its own state from a ledger of work at each branch, with no place for a person's name. Work is weighed by whether the world asked for it. The read shows which branches are growing or withering, which should split or merge, where work keeps passing between branches, and where it's drifting from what people say. People sense too, together, in the Field. No reading can decide, pay or seat anyone.

**Steadying, repair and learning.** A reading out of range becomes work for the branch it concerns. If it stays out of range it rises one branch up, and clears only after it's been back in range for a while. An empty seat passes its weight to the branch above until someone steps in. Changes are looked back on, corrections become playbooks, and a founder's vision is tested as bets that say what would show them wrong. Put together, the organisation senses, steadies, repairs, learns and adapts: an organism powered by people and AI at once. That's as close to alive as we dare claim.

**A whole without a centre.** Source theory, the viable system model and others say something has to hold the whole. Here it's held by purpose, by the root, by an open reading of every branch's state, and by tensions that climb to the forum that can act on them, rather than by a person at the top.

**Birth, division and death.** Organisations begin from a purpose. Branches are born from the work, split when they hold two things, and fold when they're done, with nothing dropped. Endings are designed in, not avoided.

**Rehearsing the future.** Before a change is lived, organisations can be grown by simulation, with simulated people in a simulated world over many days, to see where a structure creates friction or freedom. It finds flaws, not winners.

## What's still open

How far a rehearsal on simulated people travels to real ones is unknown. Some of what holds the whole, like a fire alarm for emergencies with no override, is designed but not built. Whether an organisation deserves the word alive in a biologist's strict sense, we're happy to leave open.
