# Chapter 20. Birth, division and death

*A working draft, November 2026. This book is being written while The Grove is being built; expect it to change. Checked against the build as it stood on 1 November 2026.*

---

**In this chapter**

- Why organisations avoid endings, and what hidden endings cost the business and its people
- How an organisation begins, and why a starter pack describes but creates nothing
- How branches are born, split and folded, with nothing dropped when one ends
- How people leave or step down, and how locks protect value and those who depend on it
- What we've designed but not built for whole organisations that divide, merge or close

---

There's a belief so deep in organisational life that it rarely gets said out loud. A good organisation lasts for ever.

The sceptic holds it without needing to defend it. Growth is success. Splitting means something went wrong: a falling out, a failed merger, a team that couldn't get on. Closing is the worst failure of all. And whenever a part of an organisation ends, or a person leaves, someone loses. Their job, their standing, the thing they built. So the sensible move is to keep everything going for as long as possible, and to treat every ending as damage to be limited.

It's a fair belief, and it has history behind it. Endings in most organisations really are painful, and the people who carry the pain are rarely the ones who decided. If every ending is a loss, avoiding them looks like kindness.

This chapter opens the last part of the book, which looks beyond a single organisation. It starts with the edges of one: how an organisation on The Grove begins, how its parts divide and end, how a person leaves, and what we've designed, but not yet built, for whole organisations that split, join or close.

## What avoiding endings costs

Watch what organisations do instead of ending things.

A team that has done its job isn't closed. It's kept on a budget line, given a new name, handed a vague remit, and slowly starved. A department that has grown into two different kinds of work isn't divided by the people in it. It's restructured from above, usually in one go, usually after years of strain. A project everyone knows is finished stays open because closing it would mean admitting it's finished. And when the money tightens, the ending that was avoided for years arrives all at once, as layoffs: decided in a small room, announced on a Tuesday, carried out by people who had no say.

The business cost is drag. Effort goes into keeping alive what no longer earns its place, while new work waits for a home. The organisation carries its own dead weight, and the people inside can feel it.

The human cost is worse. When endings are hidden, they can't be fair. Nobody plans a landing for the people affected, because nobody admitted there would be an ending. The person whose role disappears in a restructure finds out with everyone else. The knowledge in a closed team scatters. And the lesson people learn is that ending is something done to you, never something you take part in.

The writer Arie de Geus spent years at Shell studying why a few companies live for centuries while most die young.[^1] What struck him was that the long-lived ones behaved like living things: they paid attention to their surroundings, let parts of themselves change, and were tolerant of activity at the edges. They didn't survive by refusing to change shape. They survived by being willing to change it. William Bridges, writing about change in organisations, made a related point from the human side: every transition begins with an ending, and organisations that skip the ending get stuck in the middle.[^2]

## Endings as part of life

Chapter 4 asked what changes if an organisation is alive rather than a machine, and one answer was that living things are born, split, merge and die, and none of that is failure. This chapter is where that picture has to show it can carry weight. If endings are part of life, then the questions change. Not "how do we avoid this?" but "who decides it, and who is looked after when it happens?"

## Beginning

An organisation on The Grove begins with one person. They give it a name, of sixty characters at most, and a purpose. By founding it they agree to the organisation's first version of how its people are here together, which they and later members can rewrite. They hold the root, the branch that holds the purpose and everything that hasn't found a home yet. In the first hour, Wise-Eye can help them draft a starter pack: the branches they mean to have, how the work runs, what they'll keep and watch. The pack describes. It doesn't create anything. A branch in it is a branch they intend, not one that exists.

That small detail matters for everything after. Nothing comes into being in an organisation on The Grove by being written into a plan. It comes into being by a decision someone can see.

## Division inside one organisation

Inside an organisation, the life of branches is built, and it runs through ordinary proposals.

A new branch is born by a proposal heard in the forum it will sit in, among its parent and its future siblings. It can start from nothing, or it can carry jobs out of its parent, taking their work and records with it. One job that has become two can be split into two. A job can move to another branch where that work already lives. And a branch can be folded back into its parent.

Folding is how a branch ends, and it's worth being exact about what happens, because this is where most organisations lose things. When a fold is agreed in the parent's forum, everything the branch answered for goes up to the parent, with its open work, its records, its domains and their connections, its playbooks and its measures. Where the parent already answers for the same job, the work joins the parent's own, so nothing is held twice. The promises it was keeping go up too, along with what it holds, money owed to it, sendings under way and the bets it had placed. What's left of its budget goes with its work. Any branch beneath it now grows under the parent. Practices that held only on that branch end, each one saying why. An open seat it had posted closes. Its holders step down. Then the branch is composted. Its name stays in the record, so the history can still say where things were.

Nothing is dropped on the floor. The work doesn't end because the branch did. It moves to where it can still be done.

The organisation's reading, described in Chapter 16, can show that a branch may be withering, or that two branches pass work back and forth so much they may want to be one. The reading never acts on that. A person raises the proposal, and the forum decides. And quiet alone is never read as dying, because some parts of an organisation are meant to be quiet.

## Leaving

For a person, the edge is simpler. Anyone can leave, at any time, with nobody's permission. Chapter 9 described what that means: off every seat, the work in your hands back to its branch, your record kept, no share of what the organisation holds, and a door left open to be invited back.

Stepping down from a seat without leaving is gentler still. You give notice, up to sixty days, and the seat stays yours through it. A shared seat carries on with the others. A seat that empties falls to the branch above, which holds it meanwhile, so the work isn't orphaned. When the root's last holder leaves, it passes to the holders of the branches just beneath it, or else to whoever has been a member longest. That rule is still provisional.

There are two places where the door narrows, and both are honest. The last member can't leave, because that wouldn't be leaving; it would be closing the organisation. And nobody can step down from the last seat anyone holds, because that would leave an organisation where nobody could decide anything. Both are refusals to strand the organisation, not to hold the person.

Exit costs nothing, and that's deliberate. Albert Hirschman called exit and voice the two ways people respond when an organisation declines: they speak up, or they go.[^3] Where voice is wide and exit is free, a person's leaving is information, not betrayal.

## Who is looked after

The fear behind the sceptic's belief is that when something ends, someone loses. Two locks, described in Chapter 15, are where that fear is met.

The first protects value. A thing held to the organisation's purpose can never be sold, given away, scrapped or written off, even if every member agrees. It can move between branches, still locked. So when a branch is folded, what it held to the purpose goes up with it, still locked. An ending can't become a way of quietly stripping the organisation.

The second protects people. Anyone can say they depend on something the organisation holds: a home, a tool they earn their living with, a supply, a way in. That entry stands in the open. A proposal to dispose of the thing can't conclude unless it says, for every person listed, how what they rely on will land. The entry survives even if they leave the organisation, because leaving doesn't stop someone needing the roof over their head. The system checks that every landing is there. Whether a landing is good enough is for the forum and the people who depend on it.

That's the difference from a layoff. The ending is decided in the open, by the people who carry the work, and nothing a person depends on can be taken away without a written answer to "and what happens to them?"

## Beyond one organisation

Here we have to be plain about what's built and what isn't.

Everything above, inside one organisation, is built. What happens when a whole organisation divides, spins a part off, joins another, or closes is designed, and only partly written.

What we've designed is this. A branch can be twinned: a sibling that tries a different approach, with a hypothesis, a measure it sets itself and a review date, and when one approach wins, the other folds into it, so nothing it learned is lost. A branch that has become a venture of its own can be spun off into an organisation, and the parent carries on. An organisation pulling in two directions can divide into two, and neither half is the original. Organisations can merge in three depths: allying while staying separate, federating under a shared root while each runs itself, or joining fully into one.

Division and merging are both cooperative. Nobody can force a split, and nobody can force a join. A division is raised as a proposal, advised by everyone affected and reshaped to meet their concerns. A division is about work, not about two teams dividing spoils: it says these streams of work have grown apart, and what the organisation holds follows the work that needs it, guided by who depends on what. People aren't assigned to a side. They choose, and they can hold seats on both. Purpose locks travel with what they lock. The landings hold. If a division can't be agreed, it lapses, and the people stay responsible for what follows. Only as a last resort, where the forum is evenly split and lapsing would itself do harm, would a jury drawn at random rule on whether an objection is legitimate. Never on who's right.

The core of The Grove does hold an earlier version of the arithmetic for splitting and federating whole organisations, tested to show that no value leaks and every lock holds. Nothing in the running system calls it, and it carries an older idea we've since dropped. Organisations can't yet divide, spin off or merge.

And closing an organisation isn't built at all. We've said it's a different act from leaving. We haven't yet designed how it's done.

## How we decided

We began with the word "fork", borrowed from software, where anyone can copy the code and go their own way. In September 2026 we retired it, because it bundled two different acts. Copying the pattern of an organisation takes nothing from anyone. Dividing what it holds takes a great deal. Treating them as one made both harder to think about.

The biggest choice was to drop a right we'd first given groups: a faction could leave an organisation and take a fair share of its money with it, without the others agreeing. It looked like protection against capture. We judged it would invite the opposite: join, split, walk off with a slice. So only a person's exit is unconditional, and dividing what's shared needs consent. We accepted a real cost. In an organisation that has genuinely been captured, the good people can leave with their identity and record, but not with a share of what they built. The purpose lock still stops anyone draining it.

The fold was built later than the rest. Simulated organisations showed the reading could say a branch wanted to merge or was withering, and nothing could act on it; the work was moved by hand and an empty branch was left behind. A fold that carries everything in one change was the answer.

We decided never to suggest merging two branches because the same people hold both. And we decided to treat a division as a passage people grieve through, not only a mechanism.

What's still open is a lot. Whether a spin-off is decided by the forum the branch sits in, or by the root every time, since it takes value out of the organisation. How long the holding stage lasts while a legal entity divides, which takes months where the software takes minutes. How closing works. And whether, once real organisations use the fold, it will feel like a fair ending or a tidy one.

## What you might take from this

Organisations avoid endings because their endings are usually unfair: decided out of sight, landed on people who had no say. Make endings ordinary decisions, made in the open by the people who carry the work, with the work carried on and every person who depends on something given a written landing, and they stop being failure. They become the organisation changing shape, the way living things do.

An ending decided in the open, with a landing for everyone it touches, is a healthy part of an organisation's life.
