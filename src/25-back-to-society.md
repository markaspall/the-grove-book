# Chapter 25. Back to society

*A working draft, November 2026. This book is being written while The Grove is being built; expect it to change. Checked against the build as it stood on 1 November 2026.*

By now a sceptic has every right to say it.

Nice idea. Perhaps a few small organisations will run like this, the ones full of people who already wanted to. But the world won't change. The races to the bottom will carry on. The big organisations will stay big and stay bossed. Governments will move at the speed of governments. And a piece of software, however careful, isn't going to touch the climate, or the next war, or the way power gathers wherever it can.

We take that seriously, because it's mostly true. Nothing in this book changes the world on its own. Chapter 1 never claimed it would. It claimed something smaller and, we think, harder to dismiss: that organisations are where most adults learn, every working day, how power works, and that what we practise there we carry everywhere else.

So the last chapter goes back to where the first began, and asks what that practice might add up to.

## What the usual organisation teaches

Think about what a working week in a bossed organisation teaches, quietly, without anyone meaning it to.

That someone above you decides. That noticing a problem is risky if it implicates someone senior, and pointless if nobody senior is listening. That agreement is something you perform in a meeting and drop in the corridor. That a promise made to a customer is the account manager's problem. That leaving is a betrayal, so people stay and go quiet instead.

None of that stays at work. It comes home. It turns up at the residents' meeting, where the same three people decide and everyone else complains afterwards. It turns up at the ballot box, as the feeling that politics is something done to you. Albert Hirschman named the two responses people have when an organisation declines: they speak up, or they leave.[^1] Most working lives teach neither. They teach a third thing, which is to stay and say nothing.

The cost to the business is everything it never hears. The cost to a society is a population trained for compliance, asked once every few years to act like sovereigns.

## The habits that travel

An organisation on The Grove asks five things of the people in it, and each one is also a habit.

**Sensing.** Anyone who belongs can call a sensing in the Field: a question put to a part of the organisation, answered in people's own words, blind, with every voice weighing the same. Over months, people learn that what they notice is worth saying, and that a group can listen to itself without anyone being singled out.

**Raising.** Anyone holding a seat can raise a tension, and anyone can surface one through a sensing or feedback. Tensions that go unanswered climb, a forum at a time, until the whole organisation has either answered them or said plainly that it hasn't. People learn that problems don't have to be somebody's fault to be worth raising, and that a raised problem doesn't quietly vanish.

**Consenting.** Decisions are made by the people who carry the work, on a clock, with advice open to everyone and a concern required to name a harm, not a preference. Every decision comes back for review. People learn the difference between "I'd do it differently" and "this will hurt something", and they learn to live with decisions they didn't love, because there's a day when the decision is looked at again.

**Keeping promises.** A commitment to someone outside has firm steps and dates, and when one is in trouble the answer is more eyes and more hands, not more pressure on a person. People learn that their word can be held without anyone standing over them.

**Leaving freely.** Leaving needs nobody's permission and takes effect at once. People learn that staying is a choice they keep making, which changes what staying means.

These aren't exotic skills. They're what a family needs when it decides whether to move, what a village hall committee needs, what a school's governors need, what a town needs when it shares a river. Elinor Ostrom found that the commons which lasted for centuries were held by ordinary people who had learned to make and keep their own rules: to watch the resource together, to settle disputes they could all reach, to meet again.[^2] Those capacities weren't innate. They were practised.

That's the argument, and it's modest. A society of sovereigns isn't built by a manifesto. It's built by millions of people who have practised sensing, raising, consenting, keeping their word and leaving honestly, and who start to expect the same of the other places they belong. Someone who has spent two years in a forum where a concern has to name a harm will notice when a council meeting doesn't ask for one. Someone used to seeing the whole of their organisation's reading will start asking why they can't see their town's.

Margaret Wheatley, writing about organisations as living systems, argued that groups organise themselves well when information moves freely, relationships are rich and they know who they are.[^3] We'd add one thing: they also need practice. Most of us have had very little.

## When AI carries the load

The habits are old. What's new is that something can carry the work that used to crowd them out.

In most organisations, the people who hold things together spend their days chasing replies, filing, remembering what was promised to whom and drafting the same email again. In an organisation on The Grove, Wise-Eye takes much of that: it routes what arrives, drafts replies, keeps track of what's promised and says what's at risk. It acts for a named branch and says so. It earns standing one kind of work at a time, and loses it again without anyone having to remember to take it away. It never consents, never blocks and never holds a seat.

When that holds, people are left with the human part: noticing what matters, caring for each other and for the work, imagining what could be different, and deciding. That's what becomes possible when the boss isn't needed and the admin isn't either. Not a world without effort, but effort spent where only a person can spend it.

It can run the other way, and the sceptics of AI in governance are right to say so loudly. An AI that decides because it's quicker. An AI that agrees with everyone and helps the wrong things happen faster. An AI that does the noticing so well that people stop noticing for themselves, until the habits this chapter describes wither from disuse. And behind all of it, power gathering in whoever builds and runs the models.

Our answer is structural, as it was in Chapter 18: build no way for the AI to have a say, make everything it does visible, and keep its standing losable. That answers the first two risks better than the third. Whether people go on sensing when something else is reading the work for them is a question we can't settle by design. We'll only see it in how people behave.

## Committing together

Chapter 1 named the trap underneath the crises: a race to the bottom, where everyone does what nobody wants because nobody can afford to stop first. Within one organisation, people can agree to hold back together. Between organisations, the same thing needs a way to commit together.

What exists today is the ground two organisations stand on: an agreement each side decides by its own forum, seen by both, looked back on in words, and ended by either. That's useful. It isn't an answer to the race.

The answer would be many organisations pledging to act, to pay a living wage or cut what they burn, only when enough others pledge too, and switching on together so nobody moves first alone. We're building towards that, and it's an intention more than a design. Which forum can bind a whole organisation to a pledge, what counts as enough, and how a pledge is seen to be kept without organisations policing each other, are all unanswered. If this book's largest claim depends on anything, it depends on that, and it isn't built.

## What we don't know

The book has tried to say what's open where it's open. Gathered in one place, it's a long list, and we'd rather it were read than skimmed.

Most of the numbers are guesses: the lengths of the decision clocks, the fourteen days before a tension climbs, how much evidence Wise-Eye needs before it earns standing. Trust is designed to fade unless it's renewed, so nobody's standing outlives the reasons for it; in the organisations running today it doesn't fade yet, and the right rate is a genuine open question. Whether advice and sensing feel like real voice to a member without a seat, or like a suggestion box with better manners, is for members to tell us. There's no emergency path; the fire alarm is designed and not built, so in a real crisis people act first and the organisation catches up. The network beyond a single organisation, and the Steward that would look after the shared code as an organisation in its own right, are decided and not built. The everyday signature, the human hand a bank or landlord wants on a page, isn't designed at all. Whether people feel lighter without a layer of tasks, priorities and reminders, or miss it, we don't know.

And beneath all of these sits the largest. None of it has yet been lived by real organisations at scale. Every chapter has been honest about that, and this one has to be too.

## Where serious people disagree

Some of the people who disagree with us have thought about organisations longer and harder than we have, and it would be poor thanks to their work to pretend otherwise.

**Source theory** holds that every organisation has a global source, the person who took the first risk and listens for the whole, and that without one there's a vacuum. We put the whole in everyone's sight and let unanswered problems climb instead. But a founder alone is exactly the centre source theory describes, and nothing guarantees a founder lets go.

**The viable system model** says a surviving organisation needs functions that manage the present and weigh it against the future. We spread those across forums and bets. The honest gap is that no single place weighs the outside world against today for the whole, and our alarm climbs slowly where Beer's jumped the layers.

**PowerShift** keeps a permanent human Orchestrator who holds the whole and ratifies binding acts, with a kill switch outside governance. We refused both, because a standing seat is one others learn to wait for. But PowerShift is honest about what the law needs, and until our signature is designed, a fair reader could find theirs the more truthful answer.

**Sociocracy** puts everyone who belongs in a circle, so everyone has consent somewhere. We let people belong without a forum, which keeps forums small and decisions moving, at the cost that a member outside every forum has no consent anywhere.

**Co-operatives** give every member one vote and the power to remove those who run things. We have no member vote on who holds a seat; the equal voice lives in the Field and informs rather than decides. Some co-operative readers will see that as a step backwards, and they may be right.

And there's an older warning that cuts against flat organisations of every kind. Jo Freeman, writing about the women's movement, showed that groups claiming to have no structure grow hidden elites instead, the friends who decide over dinner.[^4] We've tried to answer her by making the structure explicit and visible rather than absent. Whether hidden power finds new places to hide in an organisation on The Grove is a question we'd welcome her readers asking.

## How we decided

We weighed ending with a vision: a confident picture of what a society of such organisations would look like. We chose not to. A vision is a hypothesis, as Chapter 13 argued, and a book that tested every founder's bet and then closed on an untested one of its own would contradict itself.

We weighed leaving the open questions scattered where each chapter raised them. We gathered them instead, because a reader deciding whether to try this deserves to see the whole of what we don't know at once, not discover it one chapter at a time.

And we weighed how much to claim for society. The temptation was to say more. What we believe is narrower: that the habits practised at work travel, that AI can leave people more room for them or less, and that whether organisations can commit together is the step that would turn many small changes into something larger. Each of those can be tested. None of them is proven.

## Life is an experiment

We began with that phrase, and it's the only honest place to end.

A proposal says what it expects and is looked back on. A vision is a set of bets. A branch grows where the work proves it's needed and fades where it doesn't. This book is the same. It was written in public, while the thing it describes was still being built, and parts of it will turn out to be wrong.

So here is the invitation. If you've practised sociocracy or Holacracy and seen where this will break, tell us. If you've run a co-operative and think we've given away something that mattered, tell us. If you study the viable system model, or AI, or commons, and see the flaw we can't, tell us that too. The book will change when you do, and say so.

## What you might take from this

When nobody needs to be the boss and AI carries the load, what's left for people is the human part, sensing, deciding, keeping their word and leaving freely, and those habits, practised at work, are how a society of sovereigns gets built.
