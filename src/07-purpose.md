# Chapter 7. Purpose: what closes, what explores

*A working draft, November 2026. This book is being written while The Grove is being built; expect it to change. Checked against the build as it stood on 1 November 2026.*

- What happens to work beside the purpose, and why purpose isn't the boundary

Walk into almost any office reception and you'll find it on the wall. A sentence about excellence, or customers, or making a difference. It was agreed at an away day, polished by a consultant, printed and framed. And on an ordinary Tuesday, nobody inside the building could tell you what it says.

So the sceptic's view is fair: purpose statements are wallpaper. They're written to sound good, not to decide anything. When a real choice comes up, about which client to take or which project to drop, nobody reaches for the purpose. They reach for the budget, the targets, or the person in charge. If that's what purpose is, building an organisation around it sounds like building a house on a slogan.

We think the sceptic is right about most purpose statements. This chapter is about why, and what happens when a purpose is asked to do real work.

## What wallpaper costs

A purpose becomes wallpaper when something else is doing its job. In most organisations, that something is a boss. Direction comes from the person above you: what to work on this quarter, what matters more than what, which opportunity to chase. The purpose statement sits alongside, harmless, because nothing depends on it.

That arrangement has costs, and they're easy to miss because they're so familiar.

The first is drift. Without a purpose that decides, an organisation follows whatever pays or whatever its loudest people want. A charity built to help one group of people finds itself running whatever programme a funder will back this year. A software company that started out to solve one problem becomes a shop for any feature a large customer asks for. Each step looks sensible. Ten steps later, nobody quite remembers why the organisation exists.

The second is the confusion of purpose with plan. Many organisations write a purpose that's really a destination: become the market leader, reach a million users, open ten sites. A destination can be reached, and then what? Or it can be missed, and then the organisation feels like a failure even when it's doing good work. Either way, a destination can't guide the hundred small choices made on the way.

The third cost falls on people. When direction comes from a person, people learn to read that person. They spend energy guessing what the boss wants, and they stop asking what the work needs. The ones who care most about why the organisation exists are often the most frustrated, because the official reason and the real one have quietly come apart.

## A seed that has to be found

As Chapter 6 described, an organisation on The Grove has no boss handing down direction. Something has to carry it instead. That something is the purpose.

This is the turn. In a self-managing organisation, purpose isn't decoration. It's the thing that tells a steward whether a piece of work belongs, whether a new part of the organisation is worth growing, whether a request should be taken up or gently set aside. If it can't do that, nothing else will, and the organisation will drift or quietly grow a boss to fill the gap.

So we made purpose the first thing, not the last. In The Grove, *the seed is the purpose*: an organisation can't be planted without at least a working one. Wise-Eye, the companion, helps a founder find it in the very first conversation.

Finding it is harder than it sounds. In one of our test conversations, a small software team brought their company onto The Grove. Their purpose didn't come from asking them to write a mission statement. It came from asking what their company's name meant. They were called Essential. Essential for what? The answer that emerged was something like this: to provide what people need, to ease their lives, and to stay close enough to see the value of it.

Notice what that sentence does and doesn't do. It doesn't name a product, a market or a size. It points in a direction, and it can keep unfolding for as long as the company exists. It also rules things out. A project that eases nobody's life, or that's shipped and never seen to matter, doesn't fit. That's a purpose doing work.

## Purpose closes, vision explores

The most useful distinction we've found is between purpose and vision.

Purpose closes. It's the organisation's identity: the thing that stays fixed while everything inside it moves. People come and go, parts of the organisation grow and fade, work changes shape week by week, and the purpose holds still so that all that movement has something to move around. It's revisited rarely, and only by proposal.

Vision explores. A founder usually arrives with a vivid picture of what they want to build. On The Grove, that picture is treated as a set of speculations: bets about how to meet the purpose, each one to be tested against the world. Chapter 13 looks at bets in detail. The point here is that they're held lightly, and the purpose is held firmly.

An example we used again and again in design: imagine an organisation whose purpose is to support neurodivergent children. Its founder has a coaching programme in mind. That programme is only the first speculation about how to meet the purpose. Perhaps it works. Perhaps the families need something else entirely: a support network for parents, training for schools, a space for the children themselves. Many shapes could fit the purpose. If the founder had written the coaching programme into the purpose, every one of those discoveries would feel like failure. Kept apart, they feel like learning.

This also changes what success means. In an organisation on The Grove, success is how efficiently the organisation meets its purpose. Not how much vision it generates, and not how much work it does. A busy organisation full of exciting plans that serves its purpose poorly isn't succeeding. A quiet one that serves it well is.

## What changes in an organisation on The Grove

Once purpose carries direction, it has to be built into how the organisation works, not just written down at the start.

**Every part has one.** The whole organisation has a root purpose. Each branch, each part within it, has its own guiding purpose, set in the forum around its parent. A branch can't be grown without one, and it's written as what the branch does for the purpose above it. So a steward holding a small part of a large organisation can ask the same question as the founder: does this belong here? The answer is nested, the way Chapter 4 described parts that are also wholes.

**Changing the root is rare and shared.** Only the root's forum can change the root purpose, and every member is asked for advice before it decides. It counts as the highest impact a change can have, so it needs two thirds of the forum's trust behind it and no concern standing. Then it waits out a timelock, three days before the change takes effect, so nobody can rewrite the organisation's identity in a hurry.

**The organisation can see its own lean.** We call this the purpose lens. The organisation's own reading, the traces its work and its conversations leave, shows each direction it's leaning and places that direction against the purpose: within it, at the edge, or drifting out.

It's information only. A persistent lean outside the purpose isn't treated as a fault. It may be the organisation sensing that its purpose is evolving. Frederic Laloux called this *evolutionary purpose*: the idea that an organisation has a sense of direction of its own, which its people can listen to rather than dictate.[^1] Whether to steer back towards the purpose or revise it is the organisation's choice. The reading never recommends one. So far the lens has been tried only in test conversations, not yet by a real organisation over months.

**Off-purpose work gets an answer, not a home.** Sometimes work arrives that plainly sits beside the purpose: a request from a customer for something the organisation doesn't exist to do. It's still answered. Nobody is left hanging. But it's marked as beside the purpose, and it never grows new structure. No new part of the organisation forms around it. If three or more off-purpose requests arrive within four weeks, it raises a question for the root: is this a distraction, or is the purpose moving?

**Purpose isn't the boundary.** It's tempting to think the purpose draws the edge of the organisation. It doesn't. Purpose says what the organisation exists to serve. The boundary, as Chapter 4 set out, says what lies where: who takes part in which decisions, who holds which seats, who spends which money. Mixing the two leads to odd results, like treating a supplier who shares your purpose as a member, or a member who questions it as an outsider.

**Value tied to purpose stays with it.** Some assets and money in an organisation on The Grove are locked to its purpose. They can't be extracted from it, even if every member agrees. Today a purpose-locked asset can move between branches, still locked, but it can't be sold. We're building towards a way to sell one, with the proceeds staying locked to the purpose so they never return to free-circulating funds. This is one of the few rules in the system that can't be voted away. We call such rules invariants. It means that what was built to serve a purpose keeps serving it, whoever holds the seats in ten years' time.

## How we decided

Four choices shaped this chapter.

The first was whether purpose should be a gate at founding. We could have let founders start without one and fill it in later, which is what most people do. Early tests settled it. In one, Wise-Eye let a destination through as a purpose. In another, it skipped purpose altogether and went straight to operational set-up: parts, seats, work. Both were treated as misses. An organisation without a working purpose has nothing to carry direction except the founder, and the founder becomes the boss by default. So nothing plants without one. Wise-Eye checks a wording once, and only if it needs it: when a method, a price or an audience is hiding in it, or it names a place to arrive rather than a direction.

The second was whether the system should correct drift. It would have been easy to build a purpose lens that warns, nudges or blocks work drifting outside the purpose. We chose information only, because a lean outside the purpose may be the purpose evolving, and only the people in the organisation can tell which. A system that corrected drift would also freeze the organisation's identity in its founding words.

The third was how easy purpose should be to change. Too easy, and it stops holding anything still. Too hard, and it becomes the wallpaper again: a sentence nobody can revise and so nobody believes. We chose a proposal decided at the root, with every member asked for advice and a timelock. We're not certain the balance is right. It may be too heavy for a three-person organisation and too light for a large one. Real use will tell.

The fourth came later, in October. For a while, purpose was also doing the work of the boundary. Thinking alongside Ted Rau's writing on organisations as social systems helped us see they're different things, and we separated them.[^2]

What's still uncertain is the hardest part: whether people will actually read the purpose lens as information rather than judgement, and whether a purpose found in one conversation will still feel true a year later. Collins and Porras argued that lasting companies keep a core purpose fixed while changing almost everything else.[^3] Henry Mintzberg showed that most real strategy emerges from what an organisation does rather than what it planned.[^4] Our design tries to hold both. Whether it does is something only living organisations can show us.

## What you might take from this

A purpose statement is wallpaper when something else carries direction. Take the boss away and the purpose has to decide, so it has to be found carefully, held firmly and changed only at the root, with everyone heard.

The founder's vision is something else: a set of bets, tested against the world and dropped without shame when they don't work.

Purpose decides; vision gets tested.
