If you've practised self-management, you know it works. You've also probably seen it wear people out. The ideas were right: authority in roles rather than people, consent rather than command, tensions as fuel, purpose as the thing everyone serves. What wore people down was holding it all up. Meetings to run, records to keep, a constitution to remember, a facilitator to keep everyone honest. When people got tired or the pressure came on, the old shape crept back, and usually through whoever cared most and carried most.

This book starts from the same ideas and asks a different question: what if the structure were held by the system itself, so people didn't have to?

## What you'll recognise, and what's different

**Branches, not roles or jobs.** The building block is a branch: a purpose, the accountabilities it answers for, the domains it controls and the practices it keeps. No person is named in it. People take seats on branches, and a crew can share one seat, so several hats are worn in the open. You'll see Holacracy's circles and roles in this, and sociocracy's nested circles. You'll also see where it parts from them.

**Forums decide.** A forum is the people who hold a branch's seat and the seats of the branches grown directly from it. A branch's remit is decided in the forum around its parent, where whoever holds the branch's seat takes part as a person. One rule stops anyone deciding alone: a forum with fewer than two distinct people doesn't decide, and its decisions pass towards the root.

**Consent on a clock.** Consent and the advice process combine, and each stage has a clock in place of a meeting. A proposal's shape is checked by someone other than the person raising it. Advice is wide and open for at least three days. The vote stays open for two days whatever happens, so nobody can pass a change the moment it opens. It passes when the people consenting carry enough of the forum's trust, a fifth, two fifths or two thirds depending on how much the change touches, and no concern stands. A concern has to name a harm. High-impact changes wait out a three-day timelock. Twenty-eight days later, everyone who took part is asked whether it worked. People can still meet if they want to. Nothing waits for them to.

**Trust, given by peers.** The weight behind consent comes from trust that peers give one another, in each forum and on each kind of matter, and can take back. There's no global score, no chain of trust and no way to buy a say. Reputation helps choose who's asked for advice. It never decides.

**Structure from the work.** In a new organisation, tensions are mostly pictures of the future, and the loudest visionary wins. So here, a founder's vision enters as bets to be tested against the world. Structure grows from the work that actually arrives: when a kind of work keeps coming with no accountability covering it, an accountability is offered, and a person raises it as a proposal. When work keeps passing between two branches, the tension is read as the map being wrong, never as a person.

**Nobody hands out work.** Work belongs to a branch, never to a person. Nobody can give work to anyone else. Everything arrives through one door and finds the branch that answers for it.

**Firm promises, gentle on people.** Commitments get a name and a day, and they're firm on the promise, never on the person. There are no alerts, no timesheets and no scores about people.

**The admin is carried.** Wise-Eye, the system's AI, sits beside every seat and carries the paperwork that used to fall on whoever held things together: routing, drafting, tracking promises, keeping the records. It has no seat and no say.

**No founder's last word.** The founder of an organisation on The Grove holds no override. The guardrails that stop the boss growing back are written into the system, not into a document people have to remember.

## What's still open

Consent goes with seats, and members without a seat can sense, advise and move work but can't propose or consent. That line is drawn on purpose, and it's also where the boss could grow back, through whoever controls who gets a seat. None of this has yet run in a real organisation under real pressure; so far it's been tested on simulated ones.
