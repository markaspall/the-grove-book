# Chapter 9. Belonging without a seat

*A working draft, November 2026. This book is being written while The Grove is being built; expect it to change. Checked against the build as it stood on 1 November 2026.*

---

**In this chapter**

- Why a member without a seat can't propose or consent, and why that line is drawn on purpose
- How joining works as a welcome and a vouch, and why leaving needs nobody's permission
- What a member without a seat can do: sense, advise, move work, see the whole
- Why consent goes with seats, and where that lets the boss grow back
- Where this sits beside sociocracy and co-operatives, and what's still open

---

Anyone who has spent time in a sociocratic circle or a worker co-operative will have a question ready by now, and it's a sharp one.

Earlier chapters said that only people who hold a seat raise proposals, and only people who hold a seat decide. So what about everyone else? A member who belongs to the organisation but holds no seat can't propose a change, can't consent to one and can't object to one. That sounds like two classes of people: those who decide, and those who are decided about. Call the first group stewards and the second members, and it's still a hierarchy. It just has nicer words.

It deserves a straight answer, and part of that answer is that the sceptic is partly right. There is a real line here, drawn on purpose, and it has a cost. This chapter says where the line sits, why we drew it there, what pushes against it, and where it still hurts.

## What the usual answers cost

Most organisations already have two classes and don't pretend otherwise. Partners and staff. Founders with shares, and everyone else with a contract. The people below the line can be consulted and surveyed. They can't change anything. The business pays for this slowly: what people see from where they stand rarely reaches anyone who can act on it. The people pay for it by learning that belonging is conditional, that you're in only as far as your usefulness reaches.

The movements that set out to fix this went in two directions.

Co-operatives gave every member an equal vote: one member, one vote, whatever capital they put in.[^1] That's a genuine floor of equality, and it matters. But in a co-operative of any size, the vote mostly lives at the general meeting. Members elect a board, the board appoints managers, and the week's decisions are made much as they are anywhere else. The equality is real, and it's periodic.

Sociocracy went the other way. Its unit is the circle, and everyone in a circle consents to the circle's decisions.[^2] Nobody outside the room makes the call for you. But to have a say you need to be in a circle, and circles tend to grow, because being in the room is the only way to be heard. A circle of twelve deciding by consent is slow, and the people who can't make the meeting lose their voice anyway.

So the usual choice is between a vote that's equal but distant, and a voice that's close but costs everyone their evenings.

## Two things that were tied together

The turn, for us, was noticing that two different things had been bundled: belonging and deciding.

Belonging is about who's in: are you part of this, and can you see what's happening and speak into it? Deciding is about who carries a particular part of the work, and lives with it if a change goes wrong.

In an organisation on The Grove these are kept apart on purpose. Belonging is a floor. It comes by welcome, not by merit, and it doesn't shrink if you're tired, or quiet, or only here for a season. Deciding follows carrying. You decide about the parts of the organisation whose work you hold.

## Joining and leaving

Joining is a welcome, not a vote. Someone who holds a seat writes a personal invitation with a note, and sends the link themselves. Whoever opens it sees who invited them and into what, reads the organisation's agreement about how we're here together, and joins by agreeing to it, or doesn't. There's no ballot and no fee.

The invitation is also a vouch, so it carries a little weight, and it's paced: after an organisation's first thirty days, someone invites only once they've been here two weeks, and no more than two people a week. In a newcomer's first month, a seat holder other than the one who invited them can question the invitation. One question alone does nothing. If a second seat holder joins it, the newcomer's membership is put on hold, and whoever vouched for them can't invite anyone for a season. It's a guard against people being brought in by the dozen to swing things. It's also, plainly, a power seat holders have over new members that members don't have over them.

Leaving needs nobody's permission: no proposal, no notice period, no trust. It takes effect at once. You come off any seat you held, and the work in your hands goes back to its branch. What you did stays in the record; you take no share of what the organisation holds; and you can be invited back. The only person who can't leave is the last member, because that wouldn't be leaving. It would be closing the organisation, which is a different act.

## What a member without a seat can do

A member can **raise what they sense**. Anyone who belongs can call a sensing in the Field: a question put to a part of the organisation, open for a window of days. Members offer into it in their own words and mark what lands with them, blind, with no names and no counts until it closes. In the Field every member's voice weighs the same, and that view, one member and one voice, is the first one the reveal shows. Whoever called a sensing can also invite people from outside by a personal link, a customer or a neighbour, whose words are shown beside the members' and never mixed in. When it closes, the person who called it draws together what was born of it: a direction for the organisation, or work sent to the branch it's for.

The Field owes most to Otto Scharmer. In his Theory U, a group that wants to see something new has to sense together before it decides anything: to look at the situation from many places at once, and to listen past what it already thinks, before a single view hardens. He calls it co-sensing, and the Field is built for that moment. It gathers before it judges, keeps names out so nobody performs, and decides nothing.

A member can **give advice**. Every proposal is visible to everyone who belongs, and while one is gathering advice, any member can give theirs or say something on it. A change to the purpose, or to how we're here together, goes further: every member is asked for advice by default, seat or none. The forum still decides, but it decides having asked everyone.

A member can **move work to where it belongs**. Ushering work to the branch it's for isn't governance, so anyone who can see it can usher it.

A member can **see the whole**. The organisation's flame, and the readings beneath it of how things are going, are open to every member, each reading opening onto what feeds it. Nothing about how the organisation is doing is kept for the people at the root.

A member can **say they'd like a seat**. When a branch posts an open seat, every member sees it and can say, in their own words, that they'd like it.

Barbara Marx Hubbard had a name for the pull behind that: *vocational arousal*, the inner urge to give your own particular gifts to something larger than yourself. She trusted it as a guide to where a person belongs in the whole, and so do we. Someone asking for a seat is telling you where they'd give.

And a member takes part in the rest of the life of the place: appreciating what someone did, coming to the Clearing, the gathering that's kept off the record.

## What they can't

A member without a seat can't raise a proposal. If they try, they're told so and pointed to the Field. They can't check a proposal's shape, consent to it, or raise a concern that holds it. They don't hold or give trust in a forum, because each forum's trust is shared among the people who hold its seats, as Chapter 11 describes. They can't take a branch's work in hand or answer its post; that belongs to whoever holds its seat, and the way in is to join its crew. And they can't invite someone new.

There's one narrow exception on concerns. Any member can say they depend on something the organisation holds. If a proposal would move something that's locked because people depend on it, those people can hold that proposal with a concern, seat or not.

## Why consent follows the seat

The reason is accountability. The people who carry a part of the work decide about it, because they're the ones who live inside the decision on Monday morning. A decision made by people who won't carry it is the old problem in a new place: authority without responsibility. Chapter 6 described the opposite failure, responsibility without authority. The seat is how we try to keep the two together.

It also keeps the organisation honest about who's carrying what. We moved work firmly onto seats after simulated organisations showed people without seats quietly doing the work while the structure stopped growing, because nobody needed a seat to get things done. So the work belongs to the seat, and if you're doing a branch's work, the invitation is to join its crew and say so. A crew isn't a promotion. It's the people doing a branch's work, sharing its one seat.

The routing rule from Chapter 8 does the rest. A forum with fewer than two distinct people in it doesn't decide; its decisions go to the next forum up that has two or more, and end at the root if none has. A forum is made of the people who hold seats in it. Members without a seat don't change that count.

## Where the boss grows back

That last sentence is where the cost lives, and it's better said plainly.

A founder with five members, none of whom holds a seat, decides alone. Every decision routes to the root, and she's the only person there. The organisation even says so, once, where deciding lives: nobody else holds a seat yet, so what she decides is decided, and every decision is kept. Her members can sense, advise, usher and watch. They can't stop her. Structurally, that's a boss with good manners.

Nothing in the rules forces her to share. The boss can grow back by default, simply because seats weren't offered. What pushes against it is the design around the rules. Branches post their own open seats, and every member sees them. The root can be shared from the first day: a group founding together names the others before planting, and they join the root's crew as they arrive. Work goes to branches rather than to people, so there's a place someone else could step into. On the foundations, the founder doesn't get to skip anyone: a change to the purpose or the agreement waits for each member's advice, or for its advice period to run out, before it's decided. And once anyone else is here at all, the agreement changes only by proposal, in the open.

None of that is a guarantee. A founder who wants to stay the boss can. What the design does is make the shape visible: anyone can see that one person decides everything, and a branch one person holds is shown beside its siblings with nothing to explain it away. Members also keep the oldest power there is. Albert Hirschman called it exit: when voice isn't enough, people leave, and their leaving tells the organisation something it needed to hear.[^3] Here exit costs no permission, and it's there every day.

## Beside sociocracy and co-operatives

It's fairest to say where we're close to each, and where we part.

With sociocracy the closeness is real. Consent rather than majority. Decisions made by the people who do the work. Seats offered, and agreed in the forum the branch sits in. Where a crew shares a seat, everyone in it takes part, much as everyone in a circle does. The difference is that sociocracy assumes belonging means being in a circle. We let people belong without being in any forum at all, and give them voice through the Field and through advice instead of a seat at every table. We think that keeps forums small and decisions moving. The cost is that a member outside every forum has no consent anywhere.

With co-operatives the difference is larger. Co-operative members can vote out the people who run things. An organisation on The Grove has no general meeting and no member vote on who holds which seat. The equal voice lives in the Field, where it informs and decides nothing. Some co-operative readers will find that a step too far, and we understand why. Our reasoning is that a periodic vote over people is a blunt tool for a living organisation, and that what it guards against, people holding power they shouldn't, is better met by seats anyone can step out of, trust that fades and has to be earned again, and decisions that come back for review. We hold that view with some humility.

## How we decided

We weighed three ways to draw the line. Every member consents to everything: equal, and unworkable past a handful of people. Every member votes on the big things: closer to a co-operative, but it brings back majority and leaves a losing side. Or consent sits with the people who carry the work, and voice is wide for everyone else. We chose the third.

The details moved more than once. In November we decided that changes to the purpose and the agreement ask every member for advice, so nobody's foundations change without their being asked. At the same time we settled that a member without a seat doesn't propose a new version of the agreement directly: they're asked for advice on every change to it, and the Field is theirs. And we drew a careful line in the words. When the organisation says someone is alone, it counts the people here, seat or none. When it works out who decides, it counts seats.

What's still open: whether advice and sensing feel like real voice to members, or like a suggestion box with better manners. Whether open seats will be offered as readily as the design hopes, or whether founders will hold on. Whether members should have some way to question a seat holder, as a co-operative member can vote one out; for now they don't, and their routes are the Field, advice and leaving. Bringing people from outside into a lone founder's Field more widely than a personal link is written down for later. And the rule that hands on the root when its last holder leaves, to the holders of the branches just beneath it or else to whoever has been here longest, is still provisional. We think the line is in the right place. Members, not we, will tell us whether it is.

## What you might take from this

Belonging and deciding are different things. Most organisations either tie them together or give one away to protect the other. Here, belonging is a floor anyone welcomed stands on, and deciding sits with the people who carry the work.

The sceptic is right that there's a line. The answer isn't to pretend otherwise, but to keep it easy to cross and impossible to hide.

Everyone who belongs can sense, advise and see the whole; the people who carry a part of the work decide about it; and the way into carrying is open to anyone who wants it.
