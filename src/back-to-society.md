# Chapter 28. Back to society

By now a sceptic has every right to say it.

Nice idea. Perhaps a few small organisations will run like this, the ones full of people who already wanted to. But the big organisations will stay big and stay bossed, and governments will move at the speed of governments. And a piece of software, however careful, isn't going to touch the climate, or the next war, or the way power gathers wherever it can.

We take that seriously, because it's mostly true. Nothing in this book changes the world on its own. Chapter 1 never claimed it would. It claimed something smaller and, we think, harder to dismiss: that organisations are where most adults learn, every working day, how power works, and that what we practise there we carry everywhere else.

So the last chapter goes back to where the first began, and asks what that practice might add up to.

## Energy with nowhere to go

Think about what a working week in a bossed organisation teaches, quietly, without anyone meaning it to.

That someone above you decides. That noticing a problem is risky if it implicates someone senior, and pointless if nobody senior is listening. That agreement is something you perform in a meeting and drop in the corridor. That leaving is a betrayal, so people stay and go quiet instead.

None of that stays at work. It turns up at the ballot box, as the feeling that politics is something done to you. Albert Hirschman named the two responses people have when an organisation declines: they speak up, or they leave.[^1] Most working lives teach neither. They teach a third thing, which is to stay and say nothing.

The usual reading of that silence is that people don't care. We think it's the wrong reading. People aren't lazy. Their will has nowhere to go. Most people carry something they would pour themselves into given the chance: an idea, a craft, a care for some corner of the world. The tower, bosses stacked on bosses, spends that energy on sign-offs, status meetings and someone else's roadmap, a little at a time, until it goes quiet. It isn't apathy. It's energy with nowhere to go.

If that's right, the shortage isn't of people who want something different. It's of somewhere to put them: a structure that holds people instead of boxing them in. The cost of the box is the familiar one, a business that never hears what its people know. The prize for a better shape is harder to measure: whatever all that stored energy would do if it had a way out.

## The habits that travel

An organisation on The Grove asks five things of the people in it, and each one is also a habit.

**Sensing.** Anyone who belongs can call a sensing in the Field: a question put to a part of the organisation, answered in people's own words, blind, with every voice weighing the same. Over months, people learn that what they notice is worth saying, and that a group can listen to itself without anyone being singled out.

**Raising.** Anyone holding a seat can raise a tension, and anyone can surface one through a sensing or feedback. Tensions that go unanswered climb, a forum at a time, until the whole organisation has either answered them or said plainly that it hasn't. People learn that a problem doesn't have to be somebody's fault to be worth raising, and that a raised problem doesn't quietly vanish.

**Consenting.** Decisions are made by the people who carry the work, on a clock, with advice open to everyone and a concern required to name a harm, not a preference. Every decision comes back for review. People learn the difference between "I'd do it differently" and "this will hurt something", and they learn to live with decisions they didn't love, because there's a day when each one is looked at again.

**Keeping promises.** A commitment to someone outside has firm steps and dates, and when one is in trouble the answer is more eyes and more hands, not more pressure on a person. People learn that their word can be held without anyone standing over them.

**Leaving freely.** Leaving needs nobody's permission and takes effect at once. People learn that staying is a choice they keep making, which changes what staying means.

These aren't exotic skills. They're what a village hall committee needs, or a town that shares a river. Elinor Ostrom found that the commons which lasted for centuries were held by ordinary people who had learned to make and keep their own rules: to watch the resource together, to settle disputes they could all reach, to meet again.[^2] Those capacities weren't innate. They were practised.

A society of sovereigns isn't built by a manifesto. It's built by millions of people who have practised sensing, raising, consenting, keeping their word and leaving honestly, and who start to expect the same of the other places they belong. Someone who has spent two years in a forum where a concern has to name a harm will notice when a council meeting doesn't ask for one.

## When AI carries the load

The habits are old. What's new is that something can carry the work that used to crowd them out.

In an organisation on The Grove, Wise-Eye takes much of the load that crowds them out: it routes what arrives, drafts replies, keeps track of what's promised and says what's at risk. It acts for a named branch and says so. It earns standing one kind of work at a time, and loses it again without anyone having to remember to take it away. It never consents, never blocks and never holds a seat.

It can run the other way, and the sceptics of AI in governance are right to say so loudly. An AI that decides because it's quicker. An AI that agrees with everyone and helps the wrong things happen faster. An AI that does the noticing so well that people stop noticing for themselves, until the habits above wither from disuse.

Our answer is structural, as it was in Chapter 22: build no way for the AI to have a say, make everything it does visible, and keep its standing losable. That answers the first two risks better than the third, which we'll only see in how people behave.

## People hold the why

There's a harder question underneath. Machines already take in more than we can and find patterns we miss, and they're getting better at making things too. As they take on more of the thinking, the analysing and even the creating, the room people used to fill with that work shrinks. Call it a squeeze on human mindshare. It's already being felt, and it will tighten.

A squeeze is also a call. If the how of the work moves to the machines, what's left for people is the why: what this is for, who it serves, whether it's worth doing at all. Meaning has never come out of analysis. It's made by people, together, out of what they care about. And thinking was never our only kind of intelligence. A surfer doesn't calculate a wave; she feels it and moves. A good facilitator knows the room has turned before anyone has spoken. Purpose, care, feeling, attunement, being present with each other, making something beautiful together: these aren't a consolation prize for losing the analytical race. They may be the parts of us most working lives have barely asked for. The futurist Barbara Marx Hubbard called the next step after the human potential movement a social potential movement: the same question about what we could become, asked of people together rather than one at a time. People who have worked on human development have long described a widening that runs the same way, from fear to love, from me to we, from surviving to alive, from a self-centred view to one that takes in the whole. Otto Scharmer calls it the shift from ego-system to eco-system awareness. Not back to some lost garden, but through to something wider.

Picture it working. People hold the purpose and the judgement; the machines carry the weight. A crew decides what's worth making and why, and finds that the making is no longer the hard part.

> *This is where I have to speak for myself, because it's a belief before it's a design. For most of my life I trusted my head, and I was good enough inside the models I'd built. The times I was at my best weren't like that. They were faster than thought: closer to riding a wave than predicting one, a kind of feeling with the people and the situation in front of me. I've come to think that's the intelligence we've barely begun to use. When machines out-think us, and they will, the advantage left to us isn't a better mind. It's a different place to stand: in the heart, in purpose, in feeling, with each other.*
>
> *I know how that can sound. But I've worked from that place, not just sat in it, and I was more useful there than anywhere my head ever took me. So when I picture what comes next, I don't picture people pushed aside. I picture people holding the why, machines carrying the how, and the two of us making things more beautiful than either could alone. That's the seed I'm trying to plant: sovereign people, in purposeful organisations, that together make a fairer society where people create from the fire in their hearts. I can't prove it. I can only build towards it, and invite you in.*

We hold this as a hope and a direction, not a finding. The same squeeze can hollow people out. Some have built who they are around the work that's going, and losing it can be a real crisis, not a liberation. Freed time can turn into distraction as easily as care. A person squeezed out of their work with nothing to hold them doesn't become more human; they become lost. That's part of why the shape of an organisation matters now: one that holds people, asks for their sensing and keeps the decisions with them gives the squeeze somewhere to go. It can't make anyone find meaning. It can leave room for it, or take that room away.

## A society that meets its own needs

Here is the picture we're reaching for. It isn't a forecast. It's a direction we're testing, one organisation at a time.

Put sovereign people at the centre: whole, free to join and leave, belonging to several organisations at once, held while they're there. Around them, sovereign organisations, each governing itself and each meeting one need. Around those, a sovereign society: its needs met from within, by many organisations working together, and owned by no one.

The needs fall into two kinds of work. There's the hard work of getting things done: food, water and power, homes and land, health, safety, money and law. And there's the soft work of holding the people who do it: care, learning, belonging, voice, resolving conflict, celebration. In this picture the two lean on each other, and organisations serve each other across the divide. A care practice holds the safety crew after a fire. Learning feeds the people growing food and building homes. Water and power keep the halls lit where people meet. Money and law lean on the mediators when a dispute comes. Local voice carries a safety warning faster than any notice board. Each line is an agreement between two organisations, each decided by its own forum, as Chapter 25 described.

The starter packs already sketch a near version: a community on its own land, a power co-operative, a neighbourhood café, a client practice for law and accounts, a practice that holds other organisations' difficult conversations. None of them is large. That's the point.

And organisations on The Grove grow the way living things do. A branch that has outgrown itself divides; a new organisation can be born from an old one carrying the whole pattern with it, as Chapter 24 described. Groves seed groves. A bakery seeds a kitchen, the kitchen a school, the school a clinic nobody rules. The long ambition is that much of what large institutions do today could be done by networks of small, self-owned organisations, each holding the full pattern, with no throne for anyone to seize.

A network like that could also do something no society has been able to do: tell us how it's doing. If each organisation can sense itself, as Chapter 21 describes, the network of them can be read the same way, without collecting anyone's records, and the read back from society that Chapter 2 hoped for begins to exist. We're building towards the first step of that, not the last.

None of this needs a revolution. Nobody has to tear the tower down. People build around it, and over time the tower matters less. We aren't protesting. We're outgrowing.

And here is the hinge, because without it the picture would be a manifesto. We're not there yet, and no early organisation changes a government. Nothing in this design makes it impossible to capture, and we won't claim otherwise. But every large, unanswerable institution was once small, and chose its shape. The ones being founded now can choose differently.

## Committing together

Chapter 1 named the trap underneath the crises: a race to the bottom, where everyone does what nobody wants because nobody can afford to stop first. Within one organisation, people can agree to hold back together. Between organisations, the same thing needs a way to commit together.

What exists today is the ground two organisations stand on: an agreement each side decides by its own forum, seen by both, looked back on in words, and ended by either. That's useful. It isn't an answer to the race.

The answer would be many organisations pledging to act, to pay a living wage or cut what they burn, only when enough others pledge too, and switching on together so nobody moves first alone. We're building towards that, and it's an intention more than a design. Which forum can bind a whole organisation to a pledge, what counts as enough, and how a pledge is seen to be kept without organisations policing each other, are all unanswered. If the society sketched above depends on anything, it depends on this, and it isn't built.

## What we don't know

The book has tried to say what's open where it's open. Gathered in one place, it's a long list, and we'd rather it were read than skimmed.

Most of the numbers are guesses: the lengths of the decision clocks, the fourteen days before a tension climbs, how much evidence Wise-Eye needs before it earns standing. Trust is designed to fade unless it's renewed; in the system as it runs today it doesn't fade yet, and the right rate is a genuine open question. There's no emergency path; the fire alarm is designed and not built. The organisation's learned sense of itself is building towards, and whether it can sense an organisation's health from shape alone, we don't know. The Steward that looks after the shared code as an organisation in its own right is decided for the day The Grove goes live, and isn't built yet; the network of organisations beyond that comes later. The everyday signature, the human hand a bank or landlord wants on a page, isn't designed at all.

And beneath all of these sits the largest. None of it has yet been lived by real organisations at scale.

## Where serious people disagree

Some who disagree with us have thought about organisations longer and harder than we have.

**Source theory** holds that every organisation has a global source, the person who took the first risk and listens for the whole, and that without one there's a vacuum. We put the whole in everyone's sight and let unanswered problems climb instead. But a founder alone is exactly the centre source theory describes, and nothing guarantees a founder lets go.

**The viable system model** says a surviving organisation needs functions that manage the present and weigh it against the future. We spread those across forums and bets, and the honest gap is that no single place weighs the outside world against today for the whole.

**PowerShift** keeps a permanent human Orchestrator who holds the whole and ratifies binding acts. We refused that, because a standing seat is one others learn to wait for. But until our signature is designed, a fair reader could find theirs the more truthful answer to what the law needs.

**Sociocracy** puts everyone who belongs in a circle, so everyone has consent somewhere. We let people belong without a forum, at the cost that a member outside every forum has no consent anywhere.

**Co-operatives** give every member one vote and the power to remove those who run things. We have no member vote on who holds a seat; the equal voice lives in the Field and informs rather than decides. Some co-operative readers will call that a step backwards, and they may be right.

And there's an older warning. Jo Freeman showed that groups claiming to have no structure grow hidden elites instead, the friends who decide over dinner.[^3] We've tried to answer her by making the structure visible rather than absent; whether hidden power finds new places to hide, her readers will tell us.

## How we decided

We weighed ending with a vision: a confident picture of a society of such organisations. We chose not to end there. A vision is a hypothesis, as Chapter 14 argued, and a book that tested every founder's bet and then closed on an untested one of its own would contradict itself. But we also weighed leaving the picture out, and that felt like a different kind of dishonesty, because the picture is why we built any of this. So it's here, set down as a direction with its hinge beside it, and followed straight away by everything we don't know.

We gathered the open questions in one place, because a reader deciding whether to try this deserves to see the whole of what we don't know at once.

And we weighed how much to claim for society. What we believe is narrower than the picture: that the habits practised at work travel, that people's energy goes quiet for want of somewhere to go, that as AI takes more of the how, people can be left holding the why or simply left out, and that whether organisations can commit together is the step that would turn many small changes into something larger. Each of those can be tested. None of them is proven.

## Life is an experiment

We began with that phrase, and it's the only honest place to end.

A branch grows where the work proves it's needed and fades where it doesn't. This book is the same. It was written in public, while the thing it describes was still being built, and parts of it will turn out to be wrong.

If the bets hold, the change is simple to say. People would no longer be a workforce, but a force for life: free to sense, to care, to create, to choose, to belong. Collaboration would be the new competition, as we said at the start. And the future would be something to look forward to again, rather than something to brace for.

So here is the invitation. If you've practised sociocracy or Holacracy and seen where this will break, tell us. If you've run a co-operative and think we've given away something that mattered, tell us. If you study the viable system model, or AI, or commons, and see the flaw we can't, tell us that too. And if you carry something you'd pour yourself into, and nowhere to put it, start small: one seed, one crew. Someday is a tower too. The book will change when you do, and say so.

## What you might take from this

As AI becomes the how, people are called to hold the why, and organisations that hold people instead of boxing them in are where that, and a society of sovereigns, can start to grow.
