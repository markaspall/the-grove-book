# Before we begin

*A working draft, November 2026. This book is being written while The Grove is being built; expect it to change.*

---

This book is being written while the thing it describes is still being built.

That's worth saying first, because most books about organisations arrive finished. The research is done, the case studies are polished, the model has a name and a diagram, and the author's job is to convince you it works. This isn't that book. It's closer to a field journal kept on the way up a mountain nobody has quite climbed before. Some chapters will be written from solid ground. Others will be written from a ledge, looking at the next stretch and not yet sure of the route.

So, plainly: this book isn't complete, and we don't claim that what's in it is right. Some of it will turn out to be wrong. We'd rather tell you that now than have you find out later and wonder what else we kept quiet.

What we can promise is that it's honest about where it stands.

## Why write it now

> *We're writing this now because the time is right. In a little over two months we built an operating system for organisations, and almost none of it is new. The answers have been around for years, in self-management, in systems thinking, in the way living things organise themselves. What's changed is that the technology to make them work is finally here.*
>
> *The hard part was never the code. It's us. If we all woke up tomorrow having somehow forgotten that hierarchy ever existed, I think we'd build something very different. We can't forget, of course. So this is one example of what we could build instead, offered in the hope that it opens a door onto what's possible.*

For years before that, the idea was mine to carry, and it was too big to hold alone. The thinking alone was a lifetime's work, and the code was more. Then the tools changed. It became possible to try an idea, throw it away and start again in days rather than years. So I brought the idea to the "we", and we started building. Years of solo work were compressed into weeks.

The result is an operating system for organisations that run without a boss. Not without leadership, and not without structure, but without someone at the top who has to know everything, decide everything and tell everyone. It's a system of means and guardrails. The means are what an organisation needs to run: ways to sense, decide, route work, keep promises and see itself. The guardrails are what stop it drifting back to the old ways when people are tired or under pressure. What it doesn't do is dictate how an organisation should be shaped. Each organisation's shape, its purpose, its parts and its agreements, is its own, held as data. We think of that as the organisation's DNA. The code underneath is only the substrate it grows on.

The system itself doesn't have a name. The first network of organisations running on it is called The Grove, and that's the name you'll see most in these pages.

We could have waited until it was finished and then written it up. We chose not to, for three reasons.

The first is that it will never be finished. A system built to let organisations sense and respond has to keep sensing and responding itself. A book that waited for the last word would wait for ever.

The second is that the thinking matters as much as the result. Every part of The Grove came from a choice between options, and most of those choices were hard. If you only see the answers, you can't judge them. So in every chapter you'll find a section called **How we decided**: what we weighed, what we chose, and why. Sometimes you'll disagree. That's the point.

The third is that we need you. The ideas in this book stand on the shoulders of a lot of people who have thought carefully about how humans organise: in self-management, in systems thinking, in biology, in economics, in the study of how people grow. Some of them will read this and see things we've missed. We'd rather hear it while there's still time to change course.

## Life is an experiment

There's a phrase we keep coming back to: *life is an experiment.*

It isn't meant casually. It's how we've tried to build. A founder's vision, in The Grove, is treated as a hypothesis, not a plan. A proposal says what it expects to change and gets looked back on to see if it did. Structure grows where the work proves it's needed and fades where it doesn't. Nothing is assumed to be true just because someone important said it.

It would be strange to write a book about that and then pretend the book itself was settled truth. So this book is an experiment too. Each chapter is our best current understanding. When we learn something that changes it, we'll change it, and say so.

## Who this is for

We've written for a particular reader, and it helps to say who.

You're thoughtful, and a little tired. You've been through restructures that changed the boxes but not the work. You've sat in workshops where everyone agreed something important and then nothing happened. Perhaps you've tried self-management, or agile, or some other promise of a better way, and come away with scars as well as insights. You don't need convincing that something is wrong. You need convincing that anything else could actually work.

You might be doing the work in a traditional organisation, and wondering why the people who know the customer best have the least say. You might be a founder who doesn't want to become the boss you left behind. You might be a coach or consultant who has seen the dark side of transformation programmes. You might be a practitioner of Holacracy or sociocracy, curious about what changes when software and AI can carry what people used to carry by hand.

Wherever you're starting from, we'll assume you're a sceptic. Each chapter begins with a doubt, stated as fairly as we can, and tries to move one step from "that can't work" towards "that might work here". If a chapter doesn't move you, it hasn't done its job, and we'd like to know.

## How the book is built

The book runs in six parts.

**Part one** looks at the world we're in: the crises that seem too big to touch, the habit of putting someone in charge, and what nature does instead.

**Part two** asks what an organisation would look like if we took nature seriously, why that needs an operating system, and where people fit.

**Part three** goes inside, piece by piece: purpose, the building blocks of an organisation, how decisions get made, how trust is earned, how work finds its way, how vision gets tested, how promises are kept, how money moves.

**Part four** is about an organisation that can see itself: how it reads its own state, how it holds the whole without a centre, the role of AI, and what it remembers.

**Part five** goes beyond one organisation: how they're born, divide and end; how they work together; how the system itself is kept out of anyone's grip; and how it meets the legal world.

**Part six** comes back to where we started: how we test all this before people live it, and what might become possible if it works.

Every chapter follows a similar shape. It starts with a doubt, looks at what the current way costs, offers something that loosens the doubt, shows what changes, explains how we decided, and ends with one thought you could take to a colleague. Footnotes point to the people whose work we've leaned on, and to further reading for anyone who wants to go deeper. You can skip them entirely and lose nothing of the argument. They're there for the curious and the doubtful.

## Two kinds of language

People who think about organisations for a living have their own vocabulary: words like holarchy, stigmergy, consent, viable systems. They're useful words. They name things that otherwise take a paragraph to describe. But they can also make a book feel like a closed room.

We've tried to keep the door open. Where a field's word earns its place, we use it, and explain it the first time it appears. Where a plain word will do, we use the plain one. If you're new to all this, you shouldn't stumble. If you've spent years in it, you shouldn't feel talked down to. We won't always get the balance right.

## A note on "I" and "we"

You'll see both, and the line between them is deliberate.

"I" is Mark: the years before The Grove existed, the experiences that shaped the question, the things that failed and the things that kept nagging. Those are one person's story, and only one person can tell them.

"We" begins the moment the idea was shared. From then on, The Grove was conceived, argued over, designed, tested and built by one human working alongside AI collaborators: in conversation, in code, in long sessions of testing and rethinking. Every decision was a person's. Very little of the work was done alone. Claiming it as "I" would be untrue.

That, as it happens, is also one of the book's arguments. An organisation where people are the spark, the ones who sense, care and decide, and where AI carries much of the load. We've been living a small version of it while writing.

## What we hope you'll take from this

Not agreement. Agreement is cheap, and it rarely changes anything.

What we hope for is that, somewhere in these pages, a question you've carried for a while gets a little sharper. That you see something in your own organisation you hadn't quite named. That you try something small and see what happens.

And if you think we've got something wrong, tell us. That's how experiments work.

## Notes and further reading

For readers who want a way into the traditions this book draws on, three starting points:

- Laloux, F. (2014), *Reinventing Organizations*, Nelson Parker: the most widely read survey of organisations that work without traditional hierarchy.
- Robertson, B. J. (2015), *Holacracy: The New Management System for a Rapidly Changing World*, Henry Holt: the practice that gave authority to roles rather than people.
- Meadows, D. H. (2008), *Thinking in Systems: A Primer*, Chelsea Green: a clear, short introduction to seeing organisations and societies as systems.
