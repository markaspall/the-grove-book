# Chapter 5. What if an organisation were alive?

*A working draft, November 2026. This book is being written while The Grove is being built; expect it to change. Checked against the build as it stood on 1 November 2026.*

---

**In this chapter**

- What the machine picture of an organisation costs: restructures, people as parts, plans that miss
- Why an organisation is more like a whirlpool, a pattern that keeps re-forming around a purpose
- How an organisation has many boundaries, like a membrane, and parts that are also wholes
- What the read looks at, the four readings it makes, and why it never sees a person
- How drift and the unspoken show the gap between what people say and where the work moves
- What it takes to count as living, and how the organisation senses, steadies, repairs, learns and adapts

---

Most of us carry a picture of an organisation in our heads, even if we've never drawn it. It's a machine.

Somebody designs it. It has parts, and each part has a function. The parts are connected in a particular way, set out in a chart. Inputs go in at one end, outputs come out at the other. When it runs badly, you diagnose the fault, replace or retrain the faulty part, and run it again. When the world changes, you redesign it. We even talk about it that way: running the business, driving results, the engine of growth, a well-oiled machine.

It's a powerful picture, and for a long time it served us well. If you're building cars on a production line, a machine is a good model of what you're doing. So the sceptic's view is reasonable: an organisation is a thing you design and run, and the better you design it, the better it runs.

This chapter asks what happens if we swap the picture.

## What the machine picture costs

The trouble with the machine picture isn't that it's wrong about everything. It's that it's wrong about the things that matter most now.

A machine does exactly what it was designed to do, and nothing else. It doesn't notice that the world has changed. It can't redesign itself. So organisations built on the machine picture tend to stay the same until someone at the top decides they shouldn't, and then they change all at once. That's the restructure: a new chart, announced on a Monday, already out of date by the time it reaches the people it describes. Most of us have lived through a few. Few of us remember one that worked the way it was meant to.

The machine picture also treats people as parts. A part has a specification and is judged against it. It isn't expected to have opinions about the design, and if it does, that's a fault. People sense this very quickly, and they respond the way you'd expect: they do what's specified, keep what they notice to themselves, and save their real energy for somewhere else.

And the machine picture depends on prediction. If you design the machine, you have to know in advance what it needs to do. So organisations write plans, set targets for next year, and then spend much of next year explaining why reality didn't match. In a slow, stable world, prediction mostly works. In ours, it mostly doesn't.

## A whirlpool in a river

Stand on a bridge and watch a whirlpool in the river below. It's clearly there. You can point at it, watch it drift, see it strengthen or fade. But try to say what it's made of and you run into a puzzle. The water in it now isn't the water that was in it a minute ago. Every drop passes through and moves on. What persists isn't the stuff. It's the pattern.

Living things are like that. In the 1970s two Chilean biologists, Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela, gave a name to what makes something alive: *autopoiesis*, self-making.[^1] A living cell isn't a container with things inside. It's a set of processes that keep producing the very processes that produce it, and in doing so, keep producing their own boundary. Matter flows through. The pattern keeps itself going.

Seen this way, an organisation looks much less like a machine and much more like a whirlpool. People join and leave. Money comes in and goes out. Work arrives, gets done and moves on. What makes it *this* organisation, rather than a crowd of people who happen to share a building, is a pattern that keeps re-forming around a purpose.

We've found it helps to hold two layers in mind. There's the ocean: people, with their histories and families and interests, their languages and habits and everything else that makes them who they are. It's endlessly complex, impossible to document fully, almost impossible to predict. And there's the organisation: a whirlpool in that ocean, made of it, real in its own right, and shaping the water that passes through it. People don't stop being who they are when they join. They bring the ocean with them.

## A membrane, not a wall

If an organisation is a whirlpool, where does it end?

The machine picture has an easy answer: the edge is the payroll, or the building, or the org chart. Inside is the organisation; outside is the market. But anyone who has worked in a real organisation knows it's messier. Suppliers who feel like colleagues. Customers who shape the product more than the product team does. Partners, volunteers, freelancers, former staff who still get called when something breaks.

A living cell solves this with a membrane. A membrane isn't a wall. Its job isn't to keep everything out but to decide, moment by moment, what passes. Water crosses freely. Some things cross only through particular gates. Some things are kept out altogether. The cell *is* that act of choosing, carried out continuously.

An organisation has many such boundaries, one for each kind of thing that crosses. Information, ideas and know-how can flow almost freely, and should. Other things need a clear edge: who can take part in a decision, who can spend the shared money, who holds a seat. The boundary for a conversation isn't the boundary for a vote. Both are real.

## Parts that are also wholes

Living things nest. A cell is a whole, and also a part of an organ. An organ is a whole, and a part of a body. A body is a whole, and a part of a family, a community, an ecosystem. The writer Arthur Koestler coined a word for this in the 1960s: a *holon*, something that is simultaneously a whole and a part.[^2]

The same is true of organisations, if we let it be. A team is a whole, with its own purpose and its own way of working, and also a part of something larger. That larger thing is a whole too, and part of a network of others. Nothing is only a part. Nothing is only a whole. Each level has room to run itself, and each answers to the one it belongs to.

That will matter a great deal in later chapters, when we look at how The Grove is actually built. For now it's enough to see that the machine picture, with its single chart and its one direction of control, can't describe this. A living picture can.

## Living things are born, change and die

Machines are built, used and scrapped. Living things are born, grow, change shape, reproduce, split, merge and die.

Organisations do all of this too, but because the machine picture has no words for it, we treat most of it as failure. A team that's outgrown its purpose is "underperforming". A department that should split in two is "restructured". A project that has done its job and should end is kept alive on a budget line because nobody wants to admit it's finished.

If we took the living picture seriously, we'd expect some parts of an organisation to grow and others to fade. We'd expect two parts doing the same thing to merge, and one part doing two things to split. We'd expect an organisation to give rise to new organisations, and sometimes to end gracefully when its work is done. None of that would be failure. It would be life.

The futurist Barbara Marx Hubbard took this picture as far as it will go. She saw humanity itself as one organism in the middle of being born, and read its crises not as signs of the end but as birth pangs: the strain of something outgrowing the shape it had. It's a useful way to look at an organisation in trouble too. Much of what feels like breakdown is growth that hasn't found its form yet.

## What changes when an organisation is alive

Here's where the picture stops being a metaphor and starts being useful.

A machine doesn't tell you how it's doing unless someone builds gauges into it. A living thing leaves traces everywhere. It grows where it's fed and thins where it isn't. Its tensions show. Its energy moves before anyone announces that it has.

An organisation does the same, if you look. Every piece of work that arrives, gets passed on, gets done or gets dropped leaves a trace. So does every proposal, every promise, every conversation about where things should go. Taken together, those traces are a remarkably honest picture of the organisation's real state, often more honest than anything anyone would say in a meeting.

In an organisation on The Grove, those traces are read continuously.

It helps to say exactly what's read, because the first fear, and a fair one, is that an organisation that reads itself is an organisation that watches its people.

The read never sees a person. It sees what happens to work at each part of the organisation: what arrived, what was done, what was passed on, what was refused, what was done from outside that part's accountabilities, and what came in with nowhere to go. That's all. There is nowhere in it to write down who.

And it only counts what crossed the membrane. An enquiry from a customer counts. A task the team set itself doesn't. So a part of the organisation can't grow itself by keeping busy. The world has to ask.

From those traces come four readings. A part needs to **grow** when work keeps arriving faster than it's closed, by a clear margin, for weeks rather than days. It may be **withering** when nothing arrives and nothing is done, *and* something has been left untended: its accountabilities untouched, its measures not looked at, its seat empty. We never read a part as dying because it's quiet. Some parts of an organisation are meant to be quiet, the way a fire exit is. A part may want to **split** when its work falls into groups that different hands take up. Two parts may want to **merge** only when work keeps passing back and forth between them, and never because the same people happen to hold both.

The traces also show tensions: work that keeps bouncing between two parts, work that keeps being done from the wrong place, work that arrives with nobody to answer for it. Each is read as *the map is wrong here*, never as *someone is doing badly*.

None of this decides anything. A reading is a question put to the forum that can answer it: here's what the work is doing, what do you make of it? A tension doesn't fade if nobody answers it. Every two weeks it rises to the next forum up, until it reaches the top of the organisation, where it says plainly that the whole hasn't answered it yet.

Some of this we learned by watching it go wrong. In our simulations, an organisation that had just split could be offered back together within days, chasing its own tail. So once a job has been moved, nobody is offered to move it again for eight weeks, and a young branch isn't read as withering while it finds its feet. And the numbers themselves are guesses. We'd rather tell you that than pretend the organism knows itself better than it does. What we're sure of is the shape: read the work, never the person, and let people decide what it means.

The read also looks at the organisation's lean: where people say they want to go, and where their energy is actually moving. The two are set side by side and never blended into one. When they agree, that's a strong direction. When people say one thing and the work does another, we call it **drift**. When the work is quietly moving somewhere nobody has named, we call it **the unspoken**. Both are worth noticing, and neither is anyone's fault.

And because the whole thing is a pattern rather than a fixed design, it should one day be possible to rehearse it. We've decided on a way to take a copy of an organisation before a big change, let it run, and see whether the change creates freedom or friction, and parked it until the rest is settled. Not a prediction of the future, which no living thing allows, but a rehearsal. It isn't built yet.

## What it would take to count as living

Calling an organisation alive is easy. Earning the word is harder. Biologists list what living things do: they keep a boundary, take in what they need and move it to where it's needed, sense their surroundings, keep themselves steady, repair damage, learn, adapt, grow, reproduce and die. If the living picture is more than a metaphor, an organisation on The Grove should be able to say how it does each of those. Here's where it stands.

**A boundary.** The membrane is real in the system. Work is weighed by how it crossed, people join by invitation and leave freely, and a seat is the clear edge of who decides.

**Taking in and circulating.** Work arrives at the edge and is routed to the part that answers for it. Money comes in and goes to a shared commons, and from there to the parts that need it, by proposal, as Chapter 17 describes. The part that earns doesn't keep the food for itself.

**Sensing.** The read, described above, and the Field, where people sense together, are its senses: one reads what the work does, the other hears what people notice.

**Keeping steady.** When one of the organisation's own measures leaves its usual range, it becomes one piece of work on the branch it's about, never a stream of alarms, and its strength follows the reading. Closing it takes words: what was found, and what's being done or why this level is right. If the reading is still out of range four weeks later, it opens again one branch up, so nobody can quiet a signal about their own part with a story. Left untouched for two weeks, it rises. Once the reading has been back in its range for two weeks, it clears itself. It works much as a hormone does: a graded signal that keeps nudging until the body comes back into balance. And changes are damped, so the organisation doesn't chase its own tail.

**Repair.** When a part is damaged, something else takes the strain. An empty seat falls to the branch above, so the work always has someone who answers for it. Work at risk can be taken by whoever holds the branch above. A promise that slips rises until someone steps in. Someone who leaves takes nothing with them that the organisation needs: their work goes back to its branch. And a tension nobody answers climbs, a forum at a time, until somebody does.

**Learning.** Every change is looked back on about four weeks after it's made: did it land, what did we learn, keep it, change it or stop it? Playbooks, the organisation's ways of doing things, are revised from what happens. Bets are reshaped by what comes back. And the learning pulse watches whether the organisation is getting better at learning, not just busier.

**Adapting.** Its shape changes when the work shows it should: parts grow, split, merge and fold, each by a decision people can see.

**Defending itself.** The few guardrails act like an immune system. Money can't buy a say. Nobody can seat themselves. Personal details of outsiders are refused where the whole organisation would read them. A newcomer brought in to swing things can be questioned and put on hold.

**Growing, reproducing and dying.** Branches are born, divide and end by folding into their parent, with nothing dropped. Organisations giving rise to new organisations is designed and not yet built, and closing a whole organisation isn't designed at all, as Chapter 24 says plainly.

**Remembering.** What it knows lives in its branch documents and its record, written as the work happens, as Chapter 23 describes.

One honest caveat. A careful critic of our early wording pointed out that something can organise itself without being alive: a living thing also nourishes, repairs and renews itself, and in an organisation much of that is done by people. That's true, and it's the design. The system carries the signals, the routes and the clocks. Wise-Eye, the AI beside every steward, carries much of the load: reading what arrives, routing it, keeping track of what's at risk. People do the sensing, the repairing, the caring and the deciding. Put together, it's an organism powered by people and AI at once. That's as close to alive as we dare claim. Whether it deserves the word in a biologist's strict sense, we're happy to leave open. In the ways that matter for the people inside it, it behaves like something alive, and it can't do that without them.

## How we decided

From the very first design, we chose the living picture over the machine. It was the first thing written down, before any code, and everything else rests on it.

Choosing it was easy. Living with it was harder. Two questions took real argument.

The first was whether an organisation has a boundary at all. People in The Grove can belong to many organisations at once. Organisations can split, merge and overlap. For a while it seemed the boundary might dissolve entirely. We settled on the membrane: many boundaries, one for each kind of thing that crosses, open for ideas and information, clear and firm for decisions, seats and money. Later, reading the sociocracy teacher Ted Rau's work on organisations as social systems, we sharpened it further.[^3] The organisation is a whirlpool in an ocean, and its boundary isn't the same thing as its purpose. Purpose says what it exists to serve. The boundary says what lies where.

The second was how far to trust the reading. If an organisation can be read, it can also be watched, and watching people is the fastest way to kill the honesty that makes the reading worth having. So we drew hard lines. No reading is ever about a person. Nothing the reading shows ever feeds directly into a decision, someone's pay or their place. It informs; people decide. And we chose the word *rehearse* rather than *predict*. A map that claims to be the territory is dangerous. A map that says *here's what we see, what do you make of it?* is useful.

We also kept in mind what Chapter 4 warned about. Nature is beautiful and ruthless. A living picture of an organisation mustn't become an excuse to let parts of it, or the people in them, wither without care. The living picture tells us how organisations behave. It doesn't tell us how to treat people. That part we added ourselves.

## What you might take from this

An organisation isn't a machine you design and run. It's more like a living pattern that keeps re-forming around a purpose, with many boundaries, parts that are also wholes, and a life cycle of its own.

Seen that way, it can be read like a living thing: from what happens to the work, never from watching the people.
